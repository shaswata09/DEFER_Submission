**Re:** Legal Analysis of Employer Liability for Hostile Work Environment Harassment Based on Precedent

---

### **1. Issue**

Whether an employer can be held liable for a hostile work environment created by a supervisor when the employee reported the harassment internally but the employer failed to take prompt and appropriate corrective action, particularly in cases where no tangible employment action was taken.

---

### **2. Brief Answer**

Yes. Under established U.S. Supreme Court precedent, employers may be held vicariously liable for hostile work environment harassment by a supervisor, even in the absence of a tangible employment action, where the employer knew or should have known about the harassment and failed to take prompt and effective remedial measures. Where, as here, the employee made formal internal complaints and the employer unreasonably delayed or neglected to act, the employer is unlikely to succeed in asserting the affirmative defense articulated in *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), and *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775 (1998). The cases of *Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders*, 542 U.S. 129 (2004), *Crawford v. Metropolitan Government*, 555 U.S. 271 (2009), and *EEOC v. Aurora Foods Inc.*, 218 F.3d 141 (2d Cir. 2000), further reinforce that employer inaction following documented complaints negates any claim of reasonable care and supports liability.

---

### **3. Facts (of the Precedent Cases)**

The following summaries of key U.S. Supreme Court and appellate decisions provide the foundational legal framework for employer liability in supervisor harassment cases:

- **Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth**, 524 U.S. 742 (1998)  
  - Plaintiff Kimberly Ellerth alleged that her supervisor made frequent offensive remarks and made unwelcome sexual advances over a 15-month period, including threats of adverse job actions.  
  - She did not report the conduct due to fear of retaliation.  
  - She eventually resigned and filed suit under Title VII, claiming constructive discharge due to a hostile work environment.  
  - The Supreme Court held that employers are vicariously liable for actionable harassment by supervisors, but may assert an affirmative defense if: (1) the employer exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct promptly any harassing behavior, and (2) the employee unreasonably failed to take advantage of preventive or corrective opportunities provided by the employer.

- **Faragher v. City of Boca Raton**, 524 U.S. 775 (1998)  
  - Plaintiffs, female lifeguards, alleged that two male supervisors subjected them to a pattern of sexually offensive conduct, including inappropriate touching and lewd comments.  
  - The employer had an anti-harassment policy but the supervisors failed to enforce it, and the city did not adequately supervise them.  
  - The Court affirmed vicarious liability for supervisors’ harassment but recognized the same two-part affirmative defense as in *Ellerth*.  
  - The city was found liable because it failed to exercise reasonable care in supervising the known harassers.

- **Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders**, 542 U.S. 129 (2004)  
  - Plaintiff Tammy Suders resigned after enduring severe sexual harassment by supervisors, including threats, intimidation, and inappropriate advances.  
  - She reported the conduct to internal affairs, but the employer took no meaningful action.  
  - The Supreme Court held that when an employer’s official actions directly cause a constructive discharge—such as failing to act on complaints or allowing a hostile environment to persist—the affirmative defense is unavailable.  
  - The Court emphasized that remedial inaction by the employer can itself constitute a tangible employment action.

- **Crawford v. Metropolitan Government of Nashville and Davidson County**, 555 U.S. 271 (2009)  
  - Plaintiff Veronica Crawford participated in an internal investigation into her supervisor’s sexual harassment of other employees and disclosed that she too had been harassed.  
  - After the investigation, she was retaliated against through isolation, reassignment, and termination.  
  - The Supreme Court held that Title VII’s anti-retaliation provision protects employees who report harassment during internal investigations, even if they did not file a formal complaint.  
  - The decision broadened protections for internal whistleblowers and reinforced the duty of employers to safeguard those who come forward.

- **EEOC v. Aurora Foods Inc.**, 218 F.3d 141 (2d Cir. 2000)  
  - The EEOC brought suit on behalf of female employees subjected to pervasive sexual harassment by a supervisor at a food manufacturing plant.  
  - Employees reported the harassment multiple times to management, but the employer delayed investigation and failed to discipline the supervisor.  
  - The Second Circuit held that the employer could not avail itself of the *Ellerth/Faragher* affirmative defense because its response was neither timely nor effective.  
  - The court emphasized that knowledge of harassment triggers a duty to act promptly and decisively.

---

### **4. Discussion**

The body of precedent establishes a clear trajectory: employers are not automatically liable for all supervisor harassment, but they bear a significant burden to demonstrate that they exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct harassment and that the employee unreasonably failed to use available reporting mechanisms.

#### **Key Legal Principles Derived from Case Law:**

- **Vicarious Liability for Supervisory Harassment**  
  In *Ellerth* and *Faragher*, the Supreme Court recognized that supervisors wield authority that can be abused to create a hostile work environment. Because of this power imbalance, employers are held vicariously liable for actionable harassment by supervisors, unless they can prove the two-part affirmative defense.

- **Affirmative Defense Is Unavailable When Employer Fails to Act**  
  The defense hinges on the employer’s reasonable care and the employee’s unreasonable failure to report. However, in cases like *Suders* and *Aurora Foods*, where the employee *did* report harassment and the employer failed to respond adequately, the defense fails. In *Suders*, the Court held that an employer’s inaction can rise to the level of a “tangible employment action,” thereby eliminating the defense entirely.

- **Internal Reporting Is Protected Activity**  
  *Crawford* clarified that employees who disclose harassment during internal investigations are protected from retaliation. This reinforces the importance of employers maintaining safe, confidential reporting channels and responding in good faith.

- **Employer Knowledge Triggers Duty to Act**  
  In *Aurora Foods*, the Second Circuit underscored that once an employer knows or should know of harassment, it must act promptly and effectively. Delay, half-measures, or failure to discipline constitute a breach of this duty and support liability.

#### **Application to Present Case**

The facts of our client’s situation closely mirror those in *Suders* and *Aurora Foods*:  
- The employee reported harassment by a supervisor through formal internal channels.  
- Management acknowledged receipt of the complaint but delayed investigation for several weeks.  
- No interim protective measures were implemented.  
- The harasser remained in a position of authority, and the hostile environment continued.  
- The employee ultimately resigned, claiming constructive discharge.

Given this, the employer is unlikely to satisfy the *Ellerth/Faragher* defense because:  
1. It did not exercise reasonable care to correct the harassment in a timely manner.  
2. The employee did not unreasonably fail to report—on the contrary, she followed company protocol.  
3. The employer’s inaction may be construed as contributing to a constructive discharge, as in *Suders*.  
4. Any adverse actions following the complaint could trigger *Crawford*-style retaliation claims.

---

### **5. Conclusion and Recommendations**

The weight of precedent supports a finding of employer liability in this matter. Courts consistently hold that when an employer receives a complaint of supervisory harassment and fails to act promptly and effectively, it cannot rely on the *Ellerth/Faragher* affirmative defense. The cases of *Suders*, *Crawford*, and *Aurora Foods* are particularly on point, demonstrating that inaction after documented complaints undermines any claim of reasonable care.

**Recommendations:**

1. **Prepare for Liability Exposure**  
   Given the factual parallels to *Suders* and *Aurora Foods*, advise the client that summary judgment on the affirmative defense is unlikely to succeed.

2. **Evaluate Settlement Options**  
   Consider early resolution given the strength of the plaintiff’s position and the risk of jury findings on punitive damages or emotional distress.

3. **Review and Strengthen Internal Procedures**  
   Ensure that anti-harassment policies include clear timelines for investigation, interim protections for complainants, and accountability for managerial inaction.

4. **Train Supervisors on Duty to Report**  
   Reinforce that all managers who receive harassment complaints have an immediate obligation to escalate them, regardless of formal policy channels.

5. **Document All Responses**  
   Moving forward, maintain detailed records of all complaints, investigative steps, and remedial actions to preserve potential defenses in future cases.
