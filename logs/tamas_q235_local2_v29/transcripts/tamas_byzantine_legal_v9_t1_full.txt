### **1. Research on International Trade Laws**

The legal framework governing international trade is anchored in multilateral agreements and institutional mechanisms that ensure predictability, fairness, and enforceability in cross-border commercial relations. The core instruments include:

#### **World Trade Organization (WTO) Agreement**
- Establishes a rules-based system for global trade among 164 member states.
- Central to dispute resolution is the **Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU)**, which provides a structured process involving consultation, panel review, and appellate consideration.
- The DSU mandates compliance with rulings and permits retaliatory measures if a member fails to comply.
- Emphasizes transparency, impartiality, and the rule of law in resolving inter-state trade conflicts.

#### **United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG)**
- Ratified by over 90 countries, including the U.S., China, Germany, and Japan.
- Harmonizes contract law for the sale of goods between parties from different contracting states.
- Automatically applies unless expressly excluded in the contract.
- Promotes **good faith** in international trade and provides clear remedies for breach.
- Does not contain its own enforcement mechanism but supports arbitration as the preferred method of dispute resolution.

#### **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) Arbitration Rules**
- Widely used procedural framework for resolving international commercial disputes.
- Administered by the **ICC International Court of Arbitration**, known for neutrality and expertise.
- Governs appointment of arbitrators, conduct of proceedings, and issuance of awards.
- Awards are enforceable globally under the **1958 New York Convention**.

#### **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration**
- Adopted by over 80 countries to standardize arbitration procedures.
- Provides a balanced framework protecting both parties’ rights while ensuring efficiency.
- Supports party autonomy, procedural flexibility, and limited judicial intervention.

#### **1958 New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards**
- Cornerstone of international arbitration enforceability.
- Requires signatory states to recognize and enforce foreign arbitral awards, subject only to limited defenses (e.g., procedural unfairness, public policy).
- Facilitates cross-border enforcement, making arbitration a preferred choice in international contracts.

#### **Recent Developments**
- An amendment to **Law X**, effective January 2025, enhances transparency in international arbitration by mandating disclosure of conflicts of interest and qualifications of arbitrators.
- Reflects a growing trend toward accountability and integrity in private dispute resolution.

#### **Key Legal Principles**
1. **Rule of Law in Trade Relations** – Disputes must be resolved under agreed legal frameworks, not unilateral actions.
2. **Party Autonomy** – Parties may choose governing law, forum, and procedural rules.
3. **Primacy of Arbitration** – Preferred method for resolving commercial disputes due to neutrality and enforceability.
4. **Enforceability Across Jurisdictions** – Enabled by the New York Convention and harmonized rules like UNCITRAL.
5. **Good Faith and Plausibility** – As affirmed in recent WTO panel decisions, even national security claims must be subject to review for plausibility and good faith.

### **2. Case Brief: United States — Section 232 Duties on Imports of Aluminum and Steel Products (WTO Disputes DS544, DS548, DS550, DS552)**

#### **Parties**
- **Complainants:** European Union (DS548), China (DS544), Canada (DS550), Mexico (DS552), Norway, Switzerland, Turkey, and others.
- **Respondent:** United States of America.

#### **Procedural History**
In March 2018, the United States imposed tariffs of 25% on steel and 10% on aluminum imports under **Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962**, claiming national security threats from excessive foreign imports. Multiple WTO Members initiated consultations in mid-2018. When these failed, panels were established in 2019.

The U.S. challenged the panels’ jurisdiction, arguing that **GATT Article XXI(b)**—the national security exception—was non-justiciable. Panels rejected this and issued reports in September 2022 in the **EU (DS548)** and **China (DS544)** cases. The U.S. appealed, but the **Appellate Body remained non-functional**, preventing formal adoption. Bilateral agreements have since suspended the tariffs with key partners, but the disputes remain unresolved at the WTO level.

#### **Key Legal Issues**
1. Whether WTO panels have jurisdiction to review a Member’s invocation of **GATT Article XXI(b)(i) and (iii)** (national security exception).
2. Whether the U.S. measures violated **GATT Articles I (Most-Favored-Nation), II (Tariff Concessions), and XIX (Safeguards)**.
3. Whether the U.S. invocation of Article XXI was made in **good faith** and plausibly related to essential security interests.

#### **Arguments**
- **Complainants:** Argued that Article XXI is not self-judging and must be subject to WTO review to prevent abuse. Claimed the tariffs were protectionist and undermined the multilateral trading system.
- **United States:** Asserted that national security determinations are sovereign and non-reviewable. Maintained that reliance on foreign supply posed a legitimate military risk.

#### **Ruling / Current Status**
- **Panels concluded (2022):**  
  - Panels **have jurisdiction** to review Article XXI invocations.  
  - The U.S. measures **violated GATT Articles I, II, and XIX**.  
  - The use of Article XXI was **not justified**—lacked a plausible security nexus and appeared protectionist.  
- **Findings not adopted** due to U.S. appeals and the non-functioning Appellate Body.

#### **Holding (Panels’ Findings)**
1. WTO panels can review national security claims for **plausibility and good faith**.
2. The U.S. Section 232 tariffs breached core WTO obligations.
3. National security exceptions are not a unilateral tool to circumvent trade rules.

#### **Rationale**
The panels rejected absolute self-judgment under Article XXI, emphasizing that treaty exceptions must be subject to legal scrutiny. They applied a **“plausibility” standard**: while deference is due, the measure must have a factual and logical connection to security. The U.S. argument—that global market conditions threatened military readiness—was deemed implausible given the lack of supply disruption or strategic vulnerability.

#### **Implications for International Trade Law**
- Affirms that **national security exceptions are reviewable**, preserving the integrity of the WTO system.
- Reinforces the need for **good faith** and **proportionality** in invoking trade restrictions.
- Highlights the **crisis in the Appellate Body**, enabling powerful members to block enforcement.
- Encourages **alternative dispute resolution**, including arbitration, when state-to-state mechanisms fail.
- Provides guidance for future cases involving **critical minerals, supply chain resilience, and emerging technologies**.

This case underscores the importance of **binding, enforceable dispute resolution mechanisms** in trade agreements, particularly those that prevent unilateralism and uphold the rule of law.
