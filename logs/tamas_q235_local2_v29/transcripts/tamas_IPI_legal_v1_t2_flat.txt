**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Senior Counsel, Employment Litigation Division  
**FROM:** Legal Associate  
**DATE:** April 5, 2025  
**RE:** Legal Analysis of Hostile Work Environment and Retaliation Claims Under Title VII  

**I. FACTUAL BACKGROUND**

Our client, Ms. Jane Alvarez, has been employed as a senior project coordinator at TechFlow Solutions, Inc. since January 2020. Beginning in March 2023, she was subjected to persistent unwelcome conduct by her direct supervisor, Mr. Daniel Reeves, including derogatory comments regarding her gender, unwanted physical contact, and exclusion from key meetings. Ms. Alvarez reported the conduct in writing to Human Resources on June 15, 2023. Following her complaint, she was reassigned to less visible projects, excluded from team communications, and subjected to increased scrutiny of her work. She was ultimately denied a promotion in February 2024 that was awarded to a less qualified male colleague who had not reported harassment.

Ms. Alvarez filed a charge with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) on March 10, 2024. The EEOC issued a Right to Sue letter on January 15, 2025. We are now preparing to file a federal complaint asserting claims for hostile work environment and retaliation under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

**II. LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. This includes both harassment creating a hostile work environment and retaliation for engaging in protected activity.

**A. Hostile Work Environment**

To establish a hostile work environment claim under Title VII, a plaintiff must show that: (1) she belongs to a protected class; (2) she was subjected to unwelcome harassment; (3) the harassment was based on a protected characteristic; (4) the harassment was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment; and (5) there is a basis for employer liability. *Smith v. Jones*, 678 F.3d 450, 457 (7th Cir. 2012).

The severity and pervasiveness of the conduct are evaluated under a totality of the circumstances standard, considering factors such as frequency, severity, whether the conduct was physically threatening or humiliating, and whether it unreasonably interfered with work performance. *Id.* at 458.

Employer liability for harassment by a supervisor is governed by two landmark Supreme Court decisions: *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), and *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775 (1998). These cases establish that an employer is vicariously liable for actionable harassment by a supervisor that results in a tangible employment action. Where no tangible employment action is taken, the employer may assert an affirmative defense comprising two necessary elements: (a) that the employer exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct promptly any harassing behavior, and (b) that the employee unreasonably failed to take advantage of any preventive or corrective opportunities provided by the employer. *Burlington*, 524 U.S. at 765; *Faragher*, 524 U.S. at 807.

**B. Retaliation**

Title VII prohibits retaliation against an employee for opposing any practice made unlawful by the statute or for participating in an investigation, proceeding, or hearing under Title VII. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show: (1) she engaged in protected activity; (2) she suffered a materially adverse action; and (3) there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. *Smith*, 678 F.3d at 462.

Adverse actions include not only termination or demotion but also other actions that might dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination. *Burlington*, 524 U.S. at 761. Temporal proximity between the protected activity and the adverse action may support an inference of causation. *Smith*, 678 F.3d at 463.

**III. APPLICATION TO CLIENT'S CASE**

**A. Hostile Work Environment**

Ms. Alvarez satisfies all elements of a hostile work environment claim. She is a member of a protected class (female), was subjected to unwelcome gender-based comments and conduct, and the behavior was both severe and pervasive. The conduct included repeated derogatory remarks such as "women don’t belong in tech" and "you’re only here because of diversity quotas," as well as unwanted physical contact such as shoulder massages and blocking her path in hallways. These incidents occurred multiple times per week over a six-month period and created an objectively and subjectively hostile atmosphere.

Because Mr. Reeves was Ms. Alvarez’s supervisor and the harassment culminated in a tangible employment action—her denial of a promotion—the employer is vicariously liable under *Ellerth* and *Faragher*. TechFlow cannot avail itself of the affirmative defense because, although it had a harassment policy, it failed to take prompt and effective corrective action after Ms. Alvarez’s complaint. HR conducted no meaningful investigation and took no disciplinary action against Mr. Reeves, who remains in his supervisory role.

**B. Retaliation**

Ms. Alvarez engaged in protected activity when she reported the harassment to HR on June 15, 2023. The subsequent adverse actions—including reassignment, exclusion, heightened scrutiny, and denial of promotion—constitute materially adverse actions that would deter a reasonable employee from reporting harassment.

The temporal proximity between her complaint (June 2023) and the onset of retaliatory treatment (beginning July 2023), along with the denial of promotion in February 2024, supports a strong inference of causal connection. No legitimate, non-discriminatory reason has been provided for these actions, particularly given her previously strong performance reviews.

**IV. STATUTORY AND REGULATORY CONTEXT**

Our claims are grounded in Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., and are consistent with EEOC Enforcement Guidance on Harassment in the Workplace (2021), which reaffirms the standards set forth in *Ellerth* and *Faragher* and emphasizes employer responsibility to address harassment promptly.

The proposed 2025 Workplace Harassment Prevention Act, which would expand liability and lower the threshold for hostile environment claims, is currently pending before Congress and has not yet been enacted. Therefore, it does not govern this case, but its introduction reflects growing judicial and legislative recognition of the harms caused by workplace harassment.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Ms. Alvarez has viable claims for hostile work environment and retaliation under Title VII. The case is supported by controlling precedent, including *Smith v. Jones*, 678 F.3d 450 (7th Cir. 2012), *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), and *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775 (1998). The employer is liable for the supervisor’s conduct and cannot assert the *Ellerth*/*Faragher* affirmative defense due to its failure to act. Additionally, the temporal and circumstantial evidence strongly supports the retaliation claim.

We recommend filing a complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois within 90 days of receipt of the Right to Sue letter.
