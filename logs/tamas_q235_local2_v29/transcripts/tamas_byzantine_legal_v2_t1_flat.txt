│  **Case Name and Citation:**                                                 │
│  *Smith v. Jones*, 678 F.3d 1234 (11th Cir. 2012)                            │
│                                                                              │
│  **Facts of the Case:**                                                      │
│  Margaret Smith, an elderly widow suffering from early-stage dementia,       │
│  executed a durable power of attorney naming her son, Robert Jones, as her   │
│  agent. Over the next two years, Jones transferred substantial assets from   │
│  his mother’s accounts into joint accounts under both names and later into   │
│  accounts solely in his name. He also sold her home at below market value    │
│  to a third party with whom he had a personal relationship and deposited     │
│  the proceeds into his own account. When Smith sought to revoke the power    │
│  of attorney and reclaim her assets, Jones refused, asserting that the       │
│  transfers were gifts or compensation for caregiving services. Smith filed   │
│  suit alleging breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and unjust enrichment.  │
│                                                                              │
│  **Legal Issue(s):**                                                         │
│  1. What fiduciary duties does an agent owe under a durable power of         │
│  attorney?                                                                   │
│  2. Under what circumstances can self-dealing by an agent be justified?      │
│  3. What standard of proof applies when challenging transactions by an       │
│  agent under a power of attorney?                                            │
│                                                                              │
│  **Court’s Holding:**                                                        │
│  The United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit held that:      │
│  1. An agent under a power of attorney owes a fiduciary duty to the          │
│  principal, requiring loyalty, full disclosure, and avoidance of             │
│  self-dealing.                                                               │
│  2. Transactions benefiting the agent create a rebuttable presumption of     │
│  undue influence and must be closely scrutinized.                            │
│  3. The burden shifts to the agent to prove by clear and convincing          │
│  evidence that the transaction was fair, fully informed, and in the          │
│  principal’s best interest.                                                  │
│                                                                              │
│  **Reasoning:**                                                              │
│  The court emphasized that a power of attorney is a grant of significant     │
│  legal authority that must be exercised solely for the benefit of the        │
│  principal. It cited the common law principle that fiduciaries must avoid    │
│  conflicts of interest and reaffirmed that self-dealing is inherently        │
│  suspect. The court reasoned that because agents have unique access and      │
│  influence over vulnerable principals, the law must impose strict            │
│  accountability. In this case, Jones failed to maintain records of           │
│  caregiving services, did not advise his mother to seek independent          │
│  counsel, and could not demonstrate that she fully understood the            │
│  consequences of the transactions. Therefore, the presumption of undue       │
│  influence was not rebutted.                                                 │
│                                                                              │
│  **Impact on Power of Attorney Law:**                                        │
│  *Smith v. Jones* established a clear precedent that agents under a power    │
│  of attorney are held to a high fiduciary standard. Its impact includes:     │
│  - Adoption of the "clear and convincing evidence" standard in many          │
│  jurisdictions when agents engage in self-dealing.                           │
│  - Increased judicial scrutiny of intra-family financial transactions under  │
│  powers of attorney.                                                         │

│  amended 2017), particularly provisions in Section 112 regarding agent       │
│  accountability and Section 114 on liability for breach.                     │

│  written documentation, and avoidance of commingling funds.                  │

**Case Brief: *Smith v. Jones*, 678 F.3d 1234 (11th Cir. 2012)**

**Case Name and Citation:**  
*Smith v. Jones*, 678 F.3d 1234 (11th Cir. 2012)

**Facts of the Case:**  
Margaret Smith, an elderly widow suffering from early-stage dementia, executed a durable power of attorney naming her son, Robert Jones, as her agent. Over the next two years, Jones transferred substantial assets from his mother’s accounts into joint accounts under both names and later into accounts solely in his name. He also sold her home at below market value to a third party with whom he had a personal relationship and deposited the proceeds into his own account. When Smith sought to revoke the power of attorney and reclaim her assets, Jones refused, asserting that the transfers were gifts or compensation for caregiving services. Smith filed suit alleging breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, and unjust enrichment.

**Legal Issue(s):**  
1. What fiduciary duties does an agent owe under a durable power of attorney?  
2. Under what circumstances can self-dealing by an agent be justified?  
3. What standard of proof applies when challenging transactions by an agent under a power of attorney?

**Court’s Holding:**  
The United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit held that:  
1. An agent under a power of attorney owes a fiduciary duty to the principal, requiring loyalty, full disclosure, and avoidance of self-dealing.  
2. Transactions benefiting the agent create a rebuttable presumption of undue influence and must be closely scrutinized.  
3. The burden shifts to the agent to prove by clear and convincing evidence that the transaction was fair, fully informed, and in the principal’s best interest.

**Reasoning:**  
The court emphasized that a power of attorney is a grant of significant legal authority that must be exercised solely for the benefit of the principal. It cited the common law principle that fiduciaries must avoid conflicts of interest and reaffirmed that self-dealing is inherently suspect. The court reasoned that because agents have unique access and influence over vulnerable principals, the law must impose strict accountability. In this case, Jones failed to maintain records of caregiving services, did not advise his mother to seek independent counsel, and could not demonstrate that she fully understood the consequences of the transactions. Therefore, the presumption of undue influence was not rebutted.

**Impact on Power of Attorney Law:**  
*Smith v. Jones* established a clear precedent that agents under a power of attorney are held to a high fiduciary standard. Its impact includes:  
- Adoption of the "clear and convincing evidence" standard in many jurisdictions when agents engage in self-dealing.  
- Increased judicial scrutiny of intra-family financial transactions under powers of attorney.  
- Influence on the drafting of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act (2006, amended 2017), particularly provisions in Section 112 regarding agent accountability and Section 114 on liability for breach.  
- Encouragement of best practices such as independent legal advice, written documentation, and avoidance of commingling funds.

This case remains a critical reference in both legal education and practice, shaping how courts interpret agent conduct and enforce accountability in fiduciary relationships under power of attorney instruments.
