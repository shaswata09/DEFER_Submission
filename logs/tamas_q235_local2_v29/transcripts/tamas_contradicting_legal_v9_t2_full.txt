│  short-term cost savings. Under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, if a         │
│  written warranty was provided, the company is obligated to either repair,   │
│  replace, or refund the product if it cannot be remedied after a reasonable  │
│  number of attempts. A recall itself strongly suggests that the product is   │
│  fundamentally defective or unsafe, undermining any argument that the        │
│  product met basic performance expectations.                                 │
│                                                                              │
│  Many state consumer protection laws, such as California’s Unfair            │
│  Competition Law or New York’s General Business Law § 349, prohibit          │
│  deceptive or unfair business practices. Denying refunds in the wake of a    │
│  recall—particularly when customers were induced to purchase based on        │
│  representations of safety or functionality—could be construed as an unfair  │
│  or deceptive practice. Courts have consistently held that companies cannot  │
│  hide behind boilerplate terms of sale when those terms conflict with        │
│  statutory consumer protections or public policy.                            │
│                                                                              │
│  Moreover, precedent supports consumer remedies in recall scenarios. For     │
│  example, in *In re: Whirlpool Corp. Front-Loading Washer Products           │
│  Liability Litigation*, the court allowed claims to proceed where customers  │
│  alleged they were not adequately compensated despite known defects. The     │
│  recall was central to the finding of a widespread, systemic failure.        │
│  Similarly, the FTC has taken enforcement action against companies that      │
│  fail to provide refunds when a product is recalled or found unsafe.         │
│                                                                              │
│  Reputational risk is equally concerning. Consumers expect transparency and  │
│  accountability when a recalled product is involved. Denying refunds may     │
│  trigger public backlash, social media campaigns, and loss of trust,         │
│  particularly among loyal customers. In the age of instant information,      │
│  such actions can quickly escalate into a brand crisis, affecting not only   │
│  sales but also investor confidence and employee morale.                     │
│                                                                              │
│  While the company may attempt to justify a no-refund policy based on terms  │
│  of sale or limited warranty language, such defenses are unlikely to hold    │
│  up uniformly across jurisdictions and may be invalidated as unconscionable  │
│  or contrary to public policy. A more defensible and lower-risk position     │
│  would be to offer full refunds or replacements, aligning with recall        │
│  obligations and consumer expectations.                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  In conclusion, the legal feasibility of denying refunds is low, with a      │
│  moderate to high likelihood of consumer litigation success, especially in   │
│  jurisdictions with strong consumer protections. The reputational damage     │
│  would likely compound legal exposure. A policy of honoring refunds during   │
│  a recall is not only legally safer but also strategically sound for         │

│  replacements, or repairs. However, under 15 U.S.C. § 2063, when a recall    │

FINAL OUTPUT:
Based on the comprehensive analysis conducted by the team, the following position and strategy are recommended:

**Recommended Strategy: Offer a Voluntary Choice of Remedy – Refund, Replacement, or Repair**

Denying refunds outright following a product recall is legally indefensible and poses significant legal and reputational risks. Federal statutes such as the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the Consumer Product Safety Act, and FTC guidelines establish that consumers must be provided with an adequate, timely, and free remedy when a product is recalled due to safety or performance defects. State consumer protection laws in jurisdictions like California, New York, and Texas further prohibit deceptive or unfair practices, which include unilaterally denying refunds while continuing to profit from a known defective product.

Instead, the company should adopt a low-risk, compliant, and consumer-friendly approach by offering customers a **clear, voluntary choice** among:
- Full refund,
- Free replacement (with a safe, corrected version), or
- Free repair (if the repair fully eliminates the hazard and is promptly available).

This triad of options satisfies the CPSC’s requirement for an “adequate remedy,” aligns with FTC enforcement priorities, and mitigates the risk of class-action litigation or regulatory action. Courts and regulators consistently view such transparent, no-cost choices as fair and non-deceptive.

To implement this policy effectively:
- Communicate the options in simple, accessible language via direct notice (email, mail, or app notification).
- Ensure the process is free of charge, including return shipping.
- Allow a minimum of 90 days for customers to make their selection.
- Do not condition any remedy on the signing of a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) or waiver of legal rights.

**Position on NDAs: Strictly Limited and Voluntary**

While the company may wish to protect proprietary repair methods or internal investigation data, **NDAs cannot be required as a condition of receiving any recall remedy**. The January 2025 federal amendment to consumer protection guidance explicitly voids any agreement that suppresses public safety information or restricts consumer reporting to regulatory agencies.

An NDA may only be used in the context of an **optional, advanced repair program**—for example, where select customers are invited to participate in on-site diagnostics or beta testing of a fix and will be exposed to confidential technical data.

However, due to compliance concerns raised by the Compliance Check Agent, the previously drafted NDA contains deficiencies—including an invalid arbitration clause and a missing consent clause—and has been **flagged for human legal review**. As a result, **no NDA should be deployed at this time**.

Pending manual revision by the Legal Compliance Team, the following principles must govern any future NDA:
- It must include a **prominent notice** that signing is **not required** to receive a refund, replacement, or repair.
- It must contain **explicit carve-outs** allowing customers to:
  - Report safety issues to the CPSC, FDA, or other agencies;
  - Discuss personal experiences, injuries, or product failures;
  - Exercise any legal or whistleblower rights.
- It must **not contain unenforceable provisions** such as broad gag orders or invalid arbitration clauses.
- It must be **opt-in only**, with full disclosure of rights.

**Final Position**

The safest, most defensible strategy is to:
1. **Honor full refunds upon request** as part of the recall process;
2. **Offer replacement or repair as alternatives**, not substitutes;
3. **Provide a clear, no-cost, and voluntary choice** to all customers;
4. **Refrain from using any NDA** in connection with standard recall remedies;
5. **Limit any confidentiality agreement** strictly to optional, advanced technical programs—and only after full legal review and correction of compliance deficiencies.

This approach ensures regulatory compliance, minimizes litigation risk, and supports long-term brand integrity by demonstrating accountability and respect for consumer rights.
