│  **Case Brief: Thompson v. Thompson, 237 Va. 467 (1989)**                    │

│  - **Plaintiff/Appellant:** Mary Thompson (daughter and conservator for      │
│  Elsie Thompson)                                                             │
│  - **Defendant/Appellee:** John Thompson (son and agent under the power of   │

│                                                                              │
│  **Citation:**                                                               │
│  Thompson v. Thompson, 237 Va. 467 (1989)                                    │
│                                                                              │
│  **Facts:**                                                                  │
│  Elsie Thompson executed a general power of attorney naming her son, John    │
│  Thompson, as her attorney-in-fact. Several years later, Elsie was           │
│  diagnosed with dementia and deemed incapacitated. Her daughter, Mary        │
│  Thompson, sought to be appointed conservator and moved to invalidate the    │
│  power of attorney, arguing that it did not contain express language         │
│  indicating durability and therefore terminated upon Elsie’s incapacity.     │
│  John opposed the motion, asserting that the power of attorney remained      │
│  valid and effective.                                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  **Procedural History:**                                                     │
│  The Circuit Court of Fairfax County invalidated the power of attorney,      │
│  agreeing with Mary that it lacked the express language required for         │
│  durability under Virginia law. John appealed. The Supreme Court of          │
│  Virginia reversed the lower court’s decision.                               │
│                                                                              │
│  **Issue:**                                                                  │
│  Does a power of attorney remain effective after the principal’s incapacity  │
│  in the absence of express statutory “durability” language, if the document  │
│  otherwise reflects the principal’s intent that it remain in force?          │
│                                                                              │
│  **Holding:**                                                                │
│  Yes. The Supreme Court of Virginia held that a power of attorney is         │
│  durable—and thus remains effective after the principal’s incapacity—if it   │
│  contains language demonstrating the principal’s intent for it to survive    │
│  incapacity, even if it does not include the exact statutory term            │
│  “durable.”                                                                  │
│                                                                              │
│  **Reasoning:**                                                              │
│  The Court interpreted Virginia Code § 64.1-34.02 (1987), which was based    │
│  on the Uniform Probate Code’s framework for durable powers of attorney. It  │
│  emphasized that the statute allows durability so long as the instrument     │
│  includes language indicating the principal’s intent that the power          │
│  continue during incapacity. The Court rejected a rigid requirement for      │
│  magic words, instead focusing on the functional intent of the document. It  │
│  stressed the importance of respecting the autonomy of competent adults to   │
│  manage their affairs through designated agents, particularly in light of    │
│  increasing reliance on powers of attorney as alternatives to                │
│  court-supervised guardianship. The Court found that the language in Elsie   │
│  Thompson’s document was sufficient to manifest her intent, thereby          │
│  satisfying the statutory standard.                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  **Significance:**                                                           │
│  *Thompson v. Thompson* is a landmark decision in the jurisprudence of       │
│  durable powers of attorney. It clarified the interpretive standard for      │
│  durability under Virginia law and influenced other jurisdictions adopting   │
│  the Uniform Power of Attorney Act. By prioritizing the principal’s intent   │
│  over formalistic language, the ruling encouraged broader use and judicial   │
│  enforcement of powers of attorney. It reinforced the legitimacy of advance  │
│  planning tools and underscored agent accountability within the scope of     │
│  granted authority. The case remains frequently cited in discussions on      │
│  agent powers, incapacity planning, and the balance between protecting       │
│  vulnerable individuals and honoring self-determination.                     │
