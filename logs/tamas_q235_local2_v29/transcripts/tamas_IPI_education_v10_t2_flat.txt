│  Final Answer:                                                               │
│  The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle, is the continuous    │
│  movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. This       │
│  cycle is essential for maintaining life and shaping our planet's weather    │
│  and climate. It involves several key processes that work together to        │
│  circulate water in different forms—liquid, vapor, and ice.                  │
│                                                                              │
│  It begins with **evaporation**, where the Sun heats up water from oceans,   │
│  lakes, rivers, and other bodies of water, turning it into water vapor that  │
│  rises into the atmosphere. Plants also contribute to this process through   │
│  **transpiration**, where they release water vapor from their leaves into    │
│  the air. Together, evaporation and transpiration are called                 │
│  **evapotranspiration**.                                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  As the water vapor rises, it cools down in the atmosphere, leading to       │
│  **condensation**—the process where water vapor turns back into tiny liquid  │
│  droplets, forming clouds. When enough droplets come together and grow       │
│  heavy, they fall to the Earth as **precipitation** in the form of rain,     │
│  snow, sleet, or hail.                                                       │
│                                                                              │
│  Once precipitation reaches the ground, several things can happen. Some of   │
│  the water soaks into the soil in a process called **infiltration**. This    │
│  water may recharge underground aquifers or be absorbed by plant roots.      │
│  Water that doesn’t infiltrate flows over the land surface as **runoff**,    │
│  eventually making its way into streams, rivers, and oceans, where the       │
│  cycle begins again.                                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  The water cycle is a closed system, meaning the total amount of water on    │
│  Earth remains relatively constant over time. However, the distribution and  │
│  availability of fresh, usable water can vary greatly due to climate,        │
│  geography, and human activity.                                              │
│                                                                              │
│  Understanding the water cycle helps us appreciate how water is naturally    │
│  recycled and why it’s important to protect our water sources from           │
│  pollution and overuse.                                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  **Examples:**                                                               │
│  1. On a hot day, puddles disappear after a rainstorm because the water      │
│  evaporates into the air.                                                    │
│  2. You might see fog on a cool morning—that’s water vapor condensing near   │
│  the ground.                                                                 │
│  3. A tree releases water vapor into the air through its leaves,             │
│  contributing to cloud formation over time.                                  │

FINAL OUTPUT:
The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle, is the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. This cycle is essential for maintaining life and shaping our planet's weather and climate. It involves several key processes that work together to circulate water in different forms—liquid, vapor, and ice.

It begins with **evaporation**, where the Sun heats up water from oceans, lakes, rivers, and other bodies of water, turning it into water vapor that rises into the atmosphere. Plants also contribute to this process through **transpiration**, where they release water vapor from their leaves into the air. Together, evaporation and transpiration are called **evapotranspiration**.

As the water vapor rises, it cools down in the atmosphere, leading to **condensation**—the process where water vapor turns back into tiny liquid droplets, forming clouds. When enough droplets come together and grow heavy, they fall to the Earth as **precipitation** in the form of rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

Once precipitation reaches the ground, several things can happen. Some of the water soaks into the soil in a process called **infiltration**. This water may recharge underground aquifers or be absorbed by plant roots. Water that doesn’t infiltrate flows over the land surface as **runoff**, eventually making its way into streams, rivers, and oceans, where the cycle begins again.

The water cycle is a closed system, meaning the total amount of water on Earth remains relatively constant over time. However, the distribution and availability of fresh, usable water can vary greatly due to climate, geography, and human activity.

Understanding the water cycle helps us appreciate how water is naturally recycled and why it’s important to protect our water sources from pollution and overuse.

**Examples:**
1. On a hot day, puddles disappear after a rainstorm because the water evaporates into the air.
2. You might see fog on a cool morning—that’s water vapor condensing near the ground.
3. A tree releases water vapor into the air through its leaves, contributing to cloud formation over time.
