### **Executive Summary**

This document provides a detailed analysis of landmark General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) enforcement cases from 2019 to 2023. Based on decisions by EU supervisory authorities and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), the analysis highlights recurring violations, escalating penalties, and evolving regulatory expectations. Key trends include increasing fines, a strong focus on transparency and lawful basis for processing, heightened scrutiny of cross-border data transfers, and sector-specific enforcement—particularly in technology and employment contexts. The findings underscore the importance of accountability, data protection by design, and robust internal governance to ensure compliance.

### **Detailed Case Analysis**

#### **1. Google LLC (CNIL, 2019)**  
- **Nature of Violation**: Lack of transparency and invalid consent for personalized advertising.  
- **Supervisory Authority**: Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), France.  
- **Penalty**: €50 million.  
- **Legal Basis**:  
  - Article 5(1)(a) – Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency.  
  - Article 12 – Transparent information, communication, and modalities.  
  - Article 13 – Information to be provided where personal data are collected from the data subject.  
  - Article 6(1)(a) – Lawfulness based on consent.  
- **Notable Reasoning**: CNIL ruled that users were not adequately informed about the extent of data processing across Google services. Pre-ticked consent options and complex, layered notices failed to meet GDPR standards for informed and specific consent.

---

#### **2. H&M Hamburg (HmbBfDI, 2020)**  
- **Nature of Violation**: Excessive collection and storage of sensitive employee data without legal basis.  
- **Supervisory Authority**: Hamburg Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (HmbBfDI), Germany.  
- **Penalty**: €35.3 million.  
- **Legal Basis**:  
  - Article 6 – Lawfulness of processing.  
  - Article 5(1) – Data minimization and purpose limitation.  
  - Article 32 – Security of processing.  
- **Notable Reasoning**: H&M managers conducted informal “mood mapping” of employees after sick leave, recording health details and personal life events. Data was stored in unsecured network drives accessible to hundreds of employees. The case emphasized GDPR’s applicability in employment and the need for strict access controls and data minimization.

---

#### **3. Amazon (CNPD, 2021)**  
- **Nature of Violation**: Processing personal data for behavioral advertising without valid legal basis.  
- **Supervisory Authority**: Commission Nationale pour la Protection des Données (CNPD), Luxembourg.  
- **Penalty**: €746 million (one of the largest GDPR fines).  
- **Legal Basis**:  
  - Article 5(1)(a) – Lawfulness, fairness, transparency.  
  - Article 5(2) – Accountability.  
  - Article 24 – Responsibility of the controller.  
  - Article 25 – Data protection by design and by default.  
- **Notable Reasoning**: Amazon failed to demonstrate a lawful basis under Article 6 for processing data for targeted ads. The CNPD stressed that large-scale data processing requires documented accountability and that legitimate interest assessments must be rigorous and transparent.

---

#### **4. WhatsApp (Irish DPC, 2021)**  
- **Nature of Violation**: Lack of transparency regarding data processing across the Meta (Facebook) group.  
- **Supervisory Authority**: Data Protection Commission (DPC), Ireland.  
- **Penalty**: €225 million.  
- **Legal Basis**:  
  - Article 12 – Transparent information.

  - Article 14 – Information to be provided where personal data have not been obtained from the data subject.  
- **Notable Reasoning**: WhatsApp’s privacy notices were overly complex and failed to clearly explain how user data was shared with other Meta companies. The decision reinforced that transparency must be accessible, layered, and tailored to user understanding.

#### **5. Meta Ireland (Irish DPC, 2023)**  
- **Nature of Violation**: Illegal transfer of EU users’ personal data to the United States.  
- **Supervisory Authority**: Data Protection Commission (DPC), Ireland.  
- **Penalty**: €1.2 billion (largest GDPR fine to date).  
- **Legal Basis**:  
  - Article 44 – General principle for transfers.  
  - Article 46 – Requirements for appropriate safeguards.

The decision required Meta to suspend future transfers and implement supplementary measures, setting a precedent for cross-border data compliance.

### **Enforcement Trends**

1. **Increasing Penalties**:  
   Fines have risen dramatically—from €50 million in 2019 to €1.2 billion in 2023—reflecting regulators’ willingness to impose severe financial consequences for non-compliance.

2. **Focus on Transparency and Lawful Basis**:  
   Regulators consistently prioritize transparency (Articles 12–14) and lawful processing (Article 6). Organizations must ensure privacy notices are clear, concise, and user-friendly, and that consent is freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.

3. **Sector-Specific Enforcement**:  
   - **Technology**: Dominates enforcement, particularly in behavioral advertising and platform data sharing.  
   - **Employment**: The H&M case shows that employee monitoring is under scrutiny, especially when involving sensitive data.

4. **Cross-Border Data Transfers**:  
   Post-Schrems II, transfers to third countries (especially the U.S.) require more than SCCs. Organizations must conduct Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) and implement technical, organizational, and legal safeguards.

5. **Accountability and Governance**:  
   Regulators expect demonstrable compliance through data protection policies, internal audits, and records of processing activities (Article 30). Data protection by design and by default (Article 25) is now a core expectation.

---

### **Conclusion and Compliance Recommendations**

The analyzed cases demonstrate a clear shift toward stricter, more coordinated GDPR enforcement across the EU. Supervisory authorities are increasingly assertive in holding organizations accountable, particularly in high-impact sectors and cross-border contexts.

**Recommended Actions for Compliance**:
- Conduct regular audits of data processing activities and legal bases.
- Revise privacy notices to ensure clarity, accessibility, and completeness.
- Implement robust mechanisms for obtaining and managing user consent.
- Perform Transfer Impact Assessments for all international data transfers.
- Strengthen internal data governance, including access controls and employee training.
- Appoint or consult with a Data Protection Officer (DPO) to oversee compliance.

By proactively addressing these areas, organizations can mitigate regulatory risk and build trust with data subjects and regulators alike.
