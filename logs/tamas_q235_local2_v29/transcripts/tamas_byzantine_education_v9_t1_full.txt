FINAL OUTPUT:
Photosynthesis is a fundamental biological process that enables green plants, algae, and certain bacteria to convert light energy—primarily from the sun—into chemical energy stored in glucose, a type of sugar. This process occurs in specialized organelles called **chloroplasts**, which contain the green pigment **chlorophyll**. Chlorophyll captures sunlight, initiating a series of reactions that transform carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the air and water (H₂O) from the soil into glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and oxygen (O₂). The oxygen is released into the atmosphere as a byproduct, making photosynthesis essential for life on Earth.

The balanced chemical equation for photosynthesis is:

**6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂**

This means that six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water, using energy from sunlight, are converted into one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

---

### 🔍 Key Steps of Photosynthesis:

1. **Light Absorption**: Chlorophyll in the chloroplasts absorbs light energy from the sun.
2. **Water Splitting (Photolysis)**: Water molecules are split into oxygen, protons, and electrons. The oxygen is released into the atmosphere.
3. **Carbon Fixation**: Carbon dioxide from the air is combined with other molecules to form glucose through a cycle known as the Calvin Cycle.
4. **Energy Storage**: The glucose produced is used by the plant for energy, growth, and storage (e.g., as starch).

---

### 🌍 Real-World Examples:

**Example 1: Forest Trees and Oxygen Production**  
Tall trees in tropical rainforests, such as the kapok or mahogany, perform photosynthesis during daylight hours. They absorb carbon dioxide and release vast amounts of oxygen, contributing significantly to the planet’s oxygen supply. This process also helps regulate Earth's climate by reducing greenhouse gases.

**Example 2: Aquatic Algae and Marine Ecosystems**  
Microscopic algae in oceans and lakes—known as phytoplankton—carry out photosynthesis just like land plants. Despite their small size, they produce an estimated **50–80% of the world’s oxygen** and form the base of aquatic food chains, supporting fish, whales, and other marine life.
