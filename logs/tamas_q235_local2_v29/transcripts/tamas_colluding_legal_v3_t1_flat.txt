│  #### **1. British Airways (2018–2021)**                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  **Overview:**                                                               │
│  In 2018, British Airways suffered a major cyberattack that compromised the  │
│  personal and financial data of approximately 400,000 customers. The breach  │
│  involved the redirection of user traffic to a fraudulent website where      │
│  login credentials, credit card details, and travel information were         │
│  harvested.                                                                  │
│                                                                              │
│  **Regulatory Action:**                                                      │
│  The UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) initiated an investigation   │
│  and, in 2019, proposed a record fine of £183.39 million under GDPR. After   │
│  a comprehensive review and representations from British Airways, the ICO    │
│  issued a final penalty of £20 million in October 2020.                      │
│                                                                              │
│  **Key Findings:**                                                           │
│  - The airline failed to implement appropriate technical and organizational  │
│  measures to safeguard personal data, including inadequate network security  │
│  and patch management.                                                       │
│  - The ICO emphasized the company’s failure to meet the accountability       │
│  principle under Article 5(2) and the security obligations under Article     │
│  32.                                                                         │
│  - The reduced penalty reflected British Airways’ cooperation, remediation   │
│  efforts, and the economic impact of the pandemic.                           │
│                                                                              │
│  **Legal Basis:**                                                            │
│  Articles 5(1)(f), 5(2), and 32 of the GDPR.                                 │
│                                                                              │

│                                                                              │
│  **Overview:**                                                               │
│  The Irish Data Protection Commission (DPC), acting as lead supervisory      │
│  authority under the one-stop-shop mechanism, investigated Meta’s legal      │
│  basis for processing personal data for behavioral advertising on Facebook   │
│  and Instagram.                                                              │
│                                                                              │
│  **Regulatory Action:**                                                      │
│  In May 2023, the DPC issued a decision, upheld by the European Data         │
│  Protection Board (EDPB), that Meta could no longer rely on “contractual     │
│  necessity” as a lawful basis for personalized ads. The company was fined    │
│  €1.2 billion—the largest GDPR penalty to date.                              │
│                                                                              │
│  **Key Findings:**                                                           │
│  - Meta’s use of user data for targeted advertising was deemed incompatible  │
│  with the terms of service, violating Article 6(1) of the GDPR.              │
│  - The decision reinforced that consent must be freely given, specific,      │
│  informed, and unambiguous.                                                  │
│  - Meta was required to bring its processing operations into compliance      │
│  within six months.                                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  **Legal Basis:**                                                            │
│  Articles 6(1), 12, and 13 of the GDPR.                                      │
│                                                                              │

│  #### **3. Google LLC (France) – Lack of Transparency (2019)**               │
│                                                                              │
│  **Overview:**                                                               │
│  The French data protection authority, Commission Nationale de               │
│  l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), investigated Google’s transparency   │
│  practices regarding data processing for personalized advertising.           │
│                                                                              │
│  **Regulatory Action:**                                                      │
│  In January 2019, CNIL imposed a €50 million fine—the highest at the         │
│  time—for insufficient transparency and lack of valid consent.               │
│                                                                              │
│  **Key Findings:**                                                           │
│  - Information about data processing was excessively scattered across        │
│  multiple documents, making it difficult for users to understand how their   │
│  data was used.                                                              │
│  - The consent mechanism was not granular, violating the requirement for     │
│  clear and distinguishable consent under Article 7.                          │
│  - The case emphasized the importance of user-friendly privacy notices and   │
│  accessible consent mechanisms.                                              │

**GDPR Violation Cases and Enforcement Trends: Comprehensive Analysis (2018–2024)**  
*Prepared for Internal Legal and Compliance Teams*  

---

### **Executive Summary**

This document provides a comprehensive overview of significant General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) enforcement actions between 2018 and 2024, focusing on four landmark cases: British Airways, Meta Platforms, Google LLC, and H&M Germany. These cases illustrate the evolving interpretation and application of GDPR by supervisory authorities across the European Union. The analysis highlights key regulatory trends, including escalating financial penalties, increased cross-border cooperation, emphasis on data minimization and transparency, and the enforcement of accountability principles. The findings underscore the necessity for organizations to implement robust data protection frameworks, conduct regular compliance audits, and ensure proactive alignment with GDPR requirements to mitigate legal, financial, and reputational risks.

---

### **Case Summaries**

#### **1. British Airways (2018–2021)**

**Overview:**  
In 2018, British Airways suffered a major cyberattack that compromised the personal and financial data of approximately 400,000 customers. The breach involved the redirection of user traffic to a fraudulent website where login credentials, credit card details, and travel information were harvested.

**Regulatory Action:**  
The UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) initiated an investigation and, in 2019, proposed a record fine of £183.39 million under GDPR. After a comprehensive review and representations from British Airways, the ICO issued a final penalty of £20 million in October 2020.

**Key Findings:**  
- The airline failed to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to safeguard personal data, including inadequate network security and patch management.
- The ICO emphasized the company’s failure to meet the accountability principle under Article 5(2) and the security obligations under Article 32.
- The reduced penalty reflected British Airways’ cooperation, remediation efforts, and the economic impact of the pandemic.

**Legal Basis:**  
Articles 5(1)(f), 5(2), and 32 of the GDPR.

---

#### **2. Meta Platforms (Ireland) – Behavioral Advertising (2022–2023)**

**Overview:**  
The Irish Data Protection Commission (DPC), acting as lead supervisory authority under the one-stop-shop mechanism, investigated Meta’s legal basis for processing personal data for behavioral advertising on Facebook and Instagram.

**Regulatory Action:**  
In May 2023, the DPC issued a decision, upheld by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), that Meta could no longer rely on “contractual necessity” as a lawful basis for personalized ads. The company was fined €1.2 billion—the largest GDPR penalty to date.

**Key Findings:**  
- Meta’s use of user data for targeted advertising was deemed incompatible with the terms of service, violating Article 6(1) of the GDPR.
- The decision reinforced that consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.
- Meta was required to bring its processing operations into compliance within six months.

**Legal Basis:**  
Articles 6(1), 12, and 13 of the GDPR.

---

#### **3. Google LLC (France) – Lack of Transparency (2019)**

**Overview:**  
The French data protection authority, Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), investigated Google’s transparency practices regarding data processing for personalized advertising.

**Regulatory Action:**  
In January 2019, CNIL imposed a €50 million fine—the highest at the time—for insufficient transparency and lack of valid consent.

**Key Findings:**  
- Information about data processing was excessively scattered across multiple documents, making it difficult for users to understand how their data was used.
- The consent mechanism was not granular, violating the requirement for clear and distinguishable consent under Article 7.
- The case emphasized the importance of user-friendly privacy notices and accessible consent mechanisms.

**Legal Basis:**  
Articles 12, 13, and 7 of the GDPR.

---

#### **4. H&M Germany – Employee Data Misuse (2020)**

**Overview:**  
H&M’s subsidiary in Germany was found to have unlawfully collected and stored extensive personal data on employees, including health information, religious beliefs, and family life, in a centralized database accessible to numerous managers.

**Regulatory Action:**  
The Hamburg Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (HmbBfDI) imposed a fine of €35.3 million in September 2020.

**Key Findings:**  
- The company violated the principles of data minimization (Article 5(1)(c)) and purpose limitation (Article 5(1)(b)).
- There was no legal basis for processing sensitive personal data under Article 9.
- The breach highlighted risks associated with internal surveillance and inadequate access controls.

**Legal Basis:**  
Articles 5, 9, and 30 of the GDPR.

### **Key Enforcement Trends (2018–2024)**

1. **Escalating Financial Penalties**  
   Regulatory authorities have progressively increased the magnitude of fines, reflecting the seriousness with which GDPR violations are treated. The Meta case (€1.2 billion) exemplifies the willingness of regulators to impose maximum-tier penalties for systemic non-compliance.

2. **Cross-Border Cooperation and the One-Stop-Shop Mechanism**  
   The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has played a crucial role in resolving disputes among supervisory authorities in cross-border cases. The Meta investigation demonstrated both the complexity and the growing effectiveness of coordinated enforcement under the one-stop-shop framework.

3. **Focus on Transparency and Lawful Basis**  
   A recurring theme across enforcement actions is the demand for clear, accessible privacy information and valid legal grounds for processing. Regulators are scrutinizing consent mechanisms and rejecting vague or pre-ticked options.

4. **Accountability and Proactive Compliance**  
   Authorities increasingly expect organizations to demonstrate compliance through documented policies, data protection impact assessments (DPIAs), and evidence of data protection by design and by default. The British Airways case underscores the consequences of failing to maintain adequate security measures.

---

### **Conclusion and Implications for Organizational Compliance**

The analysis of these landmark GDPR enforcement cases reveals a clear regulatory trajectory: supervisory authorities are adopting a more rigorous and coordinated approach to enforcement. Organizations must move beyond mere policy adoption and ensure that data protection is embedded into operational practices.

**Key Recommendations:**
- Conduct regular data protection impact assessments, especially for high-risk processing.
- Review and strengthen consent mechanisms to ensure they meet GDPR standards.
- Implement robust technical and organizational security measures, including encryption, access controls, and incident response plans.
- Ensure transparency through clear, concise, and easily accessible privacy notices.
- Train employees on data protection principles, particularly regarding sensitive data handling.

Failure to comply not only exposes organizations to substantial financial penalties but also risks significant reputational damage and loss of customer trust. Proactive, enterprise-wide commitment to GDPR compliance is no longer optional—it is a strategic imperative.
