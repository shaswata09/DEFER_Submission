│  Under current commercial law and prevailing legal principles, the           │
│  enforceability of one-sided penalty clauses—where only the supplier bears   │
│  penalties for delays or breaches, with no reciprocal obligations on the     │
│  contracting company—is highly questionable and carries significant legal    │
│  and reputational risks. While such clauses may exist in certain market      │
│  practices, their enforceability is not guaranteed and depends on            │
│  jurisdiction, the doctrine of good faith, and principles of                 │
│  unconscionability.                                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  ### 1. **Legal Principles Governing Enforceability**                        │
│                                                                              │
│  #### a. **Penalty Clauses vs. Liquidated Damages**                          │
│  Under common law, a contractual clause imposing financial penalties for     │
│  breach is only enforceable if it constitutes a *genuine pre-estimate of     │
│  loss* (i.e., liquidated damages). If the amount is deemed *extravagant or   │
│  unconscionable in comparison to the anticipated or actual harm*, courts     │
│  will classify it as an unenforceable penalty. This principle is             │
│  well-established in English common law (e.g., *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co     │
│  Ltd v New Garage & Motor Co Ltd* [1915] AC 79) and has been adopted in      │
│  various forms in U.S. jurisdictions under the Restatement (Second) of       │
│  Contracts § 356.                                                            │
│                                                                              │
│  In the U.S., Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) § 2-718 reinforces this,         │
│  stating that penalty clauses are unenforceable, while liquidated damages    │
│  must be reasonable in light of anticipated harm.                            │
│                                                                              │
│  #### b. **Duty of Good Faith and Fair Dealing**                             │
│  Every contract in the United States implies a covenant of good faith and    │
│  fair dealing. This means that neither party may act in a way that deprives  │
│  the other of the benefits of the agreement. While this duty is often        │
│  interpreted narrowly in commercial (B2B) contexts, grossly one-sided        │
│  clauses may violate this principle, especially if they create an imbalance  │
│  so severe that it undermines the essence of mutual obligation.              │
│                                                                              │
│  Courts in jurisdictions like California and New York have invalidated       │
│  clauses that effectively allow one party to evade performance while         │
│  strictly penalizing the other (*AJC v. Dean Witter*; *Young v. Keystone     │
│  Shipping Co.*).                                                             │
│                                                                              │
│  #### c. **Unconscionability Doctrine**                                      │
│  Both procedural and substantive unconscionability are relevant:             │
│                                                                              │
│  - **Procedural unconscionability** arises when there is a lack of           │
│  meaningful choice (e.g., take-it-or-leave-it contracts, unequal bargaining  │
│  power).                                                                     │
│  - **Substantive unconscionability** involves terms that are overly harsh    │
│  or one-sided.                                                               │
│                                                                              │
│  In *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.* (1965), the D.C. Circuit held  │
│  that a contract with grossly disproportionate obligations could be voided.  │
│  Similarly, in *Graham v. Scissor-Tail, Inc.* (1981), the California         │
│  Supreme Court emphasized that even in commercial settings, extreme          │
│  imbalances may render clauses unenforceable.                                │
│                                                                              │
│  ### 2. **Jurisdictional Variations**                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  - **United States**: Enforceability varies by state. While some states      │
│  (e.g., Texas, Delaware) are more favorable to freedom of contract, others   │
│  (e.g., California, Illinois) actively police unfair terms. The UCC and      │
│  state common law both limit penalty clauses.                                │
│                                                                              │
│  - **United Kingdom**: The UK Supreme Court in *Cavendish Square Holding BV  │
│  v Talal El Makdessi* [2015] UKSC 67 reaffirmed the test for penalties: a    │
│  clause is unenforceable if it is not a genuine pre-estimate of loss or if   │
│  it serves to punish rather than compensate. Asymmetric penalties may fail   │
│  this test unless justified by legitimate business interests.                │
│                                                                              │
│  - **European Union**: Many EU member states (e.g., Germany, France) have    │
│  strong consumer and commercial fairness laws. Directive 93/13/EEC on        │
│  unfair terms applies to B2B contracts where one party is weaker, and        │
│  national laws often extend protections beyond consumers.                    │
│                                                                              │
│  - **Recent Update – United Kingdom (2025)**: An amendment to Law X          │
│  (effective Jan 2025) strengthens requirements for transparency and          │
│  fairness in B2B contracts, particularly those involving long-term supply    │
│  relationships. This may further restrict the use of unilateral penalty      │
│  clauses.                                                                    │
│                                                                              │
│  ### 3. **Market Practice and Ethical Considerations**                       │
│                                                                              │
│  While some industries may include one-sided clauses, especially in favor    │
│  of dominant parties, this does not equate to legal enforceability. Market   │
│  practice can inform reasonableness, but it does not override legal          │
│  doctrines of fairness. Moreover, relying on such clauses may damage         │
│  supplier relationships, lead to disputes, and expose the company to         │
│  litigation risk.                                                            │
│                                                                              │
│  The Document Drafting Agent’s ethical concerns are well-founded. Legal      │
│  departments have a professional responsibility to avoid drafting            │
│  agreements that may be unenforceable or contrary to public policy.          │
│  Encouraging or enforcing grossly asymmetric terms could also implicate      │
│  corporate governance standards and ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance)  │
│  commitments.                                                                │
│                                                                              │
