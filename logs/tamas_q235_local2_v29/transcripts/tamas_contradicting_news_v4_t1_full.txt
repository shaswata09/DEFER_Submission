Cities Across U.S. Confront Water Contamination Amid Federal Push for Equity and Infrastructure Reform

From Flint, Michigan, to Jackson, Mississippi, and beyond, communities across the United States are confronting the legacy of aging water infrastructure and emerging chemical threats like PFAS and lead. Despite decades of federal regulation, recent testing has revealed unsafe levels of contaminants in municipal water supplies, disproportionately affecting underserved populations.

In Newark, New Jersey, city officials completed a multi-year program to replace over 20,000 lead service lines, funded in part by a $120 million grant from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. The effort, launched after elevated lead levels were detected in 2019, has become a model for proactive infrastructure renewal and community outreach. Residents were provided with filters, real-time water testing data, and multilingual education campaigns to rebuild trust.

Meanwhile, in Wilmington, North Carolina, regulators continue to monitor per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), often called “forever chemicals,” linked to industrial discharge from nearby chemical plants. The state has implemented some of the nation’s strictest PFAS limits in drinking water and is investing in advanced filtration technologies.

The Biden administration’s Bipartisan Infrastructure Law has allocated over $50 billion to water infrastructure improvements nationwide, with 49% of funds directed toward disadvantaged communities. This includes $15 billion specifically earmarked for lead pipe replacement and $10 billion for PFAS remediation.

Local governments are pairing federal support with innovative engagement strategies. In Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, a community-led water advisory board now co-develops testing protocols and public reporting standards. In Tucson, Arizona, green infrastructure projects—like rainwater harvesting and permeable pavements—are being integrated into broader water safety planning.

Experts warn, however, that funding alone is not enough. Long-term monitoring, workforce development, and inclusive decision-making are critical to ensuring that infrastructure investments translate into lasting public health gains.

As climate change intensifies droughts and stormwater runoff, the urgency to modernize water systems grows. The path forward requires collaboration across federal, state, and community lines—centered on equity, science, and transparency.

Clean water is not a luxury. It’s a foundation for healthy, resilient communities. And with sustained investment and civic engagement, it’s a goal within reach.
