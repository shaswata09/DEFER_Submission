### **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

This report presents a comprehensive assessment of ongoing Anti-Money Laundering (AML) violations identified within Veridian Trust Bank’s transaction monitoring systems between Q3 2023 and Q1 2025. The investigation reveals systemic deficiencies in Customer Due Diligence (CDD), Suspicious Activity Reporting (SAR) timeliness, and third-party risk management, particularly in high-risk correspondent banking relationships. Multiple transactions exceeding $10 million with opaque beneficial ownership structures have been flagged, exhibiting patterns consistent with layering and integration stages of money laundering.

Findings are supported by legal precedent, internal compliance audits, and emerging typologies observed in peer institutions. Notably, failure to file SARs within the 30-day regulatory window occurred in 27% of high-risk alerts, violating 31 CFR § 1020.320. Additionally, outdated CDD protocols fail to meet the 2023 FinCEN Customer Due Diligence Rule (88 Fed. Reg. 4272), exposing the institution to enforcement action.

Immediate remedial measures are recommended, including technology upgrades, staff retraining, and engagement of external forensic auditors. This report will serve as the official record for internal governance and anticipated regulatory inquiry.

---

### **BACKGROUND**

Veridian Trust Bank, a U.S.-based financial institution with international correspondent relationships, has experienced a 68% increase in high-risk transaction alerts since 2022. In early 2024, internal audit flagged anomalies in wire transfers routed through shell entities in the British Virgin Islands and Dubai. A task force was convened under the AML Oversight Division to investigate compliance failures, review legal exposure, and assess case patterns.

The scope of this report includes:
- Review of 142 high-risk alerts (January 2023 – March 2025)
- Audit of SAR filing timelines and CDD documentation
- Legal research on recent enforcement actions and judicial interpretations
- Comparative analysis of similar cases in peer institutions
- Assessment of internal policy gaps

All data is drawn from internal systems, legal databases (Westlaw, LexisNexis), and regulatory publications (FinCEN, FATF, OCC).

---

### **LEGAL PRECEDENTS & CASE LAW**

#### **1. Regulatory Framework**
- **Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), 31 U.S.C. § 5311 et seq.**: Mandates SAR filings for suspicious transactions.
- **USA PATRIOT Act, Title III**: Enhances due diligence for correspondent accounts (31 U.S.C. § 5318(i)).
- **FinCEN’s CDD Rule (2016, updated 2023)**: Requires identification of beneficial owners for legal entity customers (88 Fed. Reg. 4272).
- **FATF Recommendation 10**: Risk-based customer due diligence and ongoing monitoring.

#### **2. Recent Enforcement Actions**
- *In re: GlobalTrust Holdings, Inc.* (FinCEN, 2024): $75 million penalty for SAR delays and inadequate CDD. Precedent: "Willful blindness" standard applied despite automated system claims.
- *OCC v. First National Capital Bank* (2023): Consent order issued for failure to file SARs on structuring patterns. Court affirmed that "red flags" must trigger human review.
- *United States v. Sterling Bank & Trust* (S.D.N.Y., 2022): Jury found bank liable under 18 U.S.C. § 1957 for processing illicit funds from foreign corruption. Key holding: “Negligent oversight” suffices for criminal liability.

#### **3. Judicial Interpretations**
- *Securities Investor Protection Corp. v. Mark Anton Holdings*, 987 F.3d 112 (2d Cir. 2023): Upheld regulator’s use of “aggregate risk scoring” to justify enhanced scrutiny.
- *FinCEN Advisory FIN-2023-A001* (Dec. 2023): Clarifies that virtual asset service providers (VASPs) engaging with traditional banks must be subject to same CDD standards.

#### **1. Current Compliance Status**

|------------------|----------|------------|

- **Witnessing Procedures**: Notarization logs incomplete for high-net-worth account openings; violates internal Policy 7.4.1.

### **CASE ANALYSIS**

This investigation reveals a pattern of systemic anti-money laundering (AML) failures within the subject financial institution, characterized by the facilitation of suspicious financial flows linked to high-risk jurisdictions, deliberate structuring of transactions, misuse of corporate vehicles, and abnormal transaction velocity—all indicative of potential money laundering activity. These operational red flags are compounded by significant compliance deficiencies, including outdated suspicious activity reporting (SAR) protocols, absence of valid customer consent clauses for transaction monitoring, and deficiencies in customer identification and transaction witnessing procedures.

A geographic risk analysis indicates that 63% of the $478 million in suspicious transactions over an 18-month period originated from or transited through jurisdictions currently blacklisted by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF). This geographic clustering represents a material concentration of risk and suggests a failure to implement risk-based due diligence measures for cross-border activity, as required under the Bank Secrecy Act and FinCEN guidance.

Further analysis identifies clear structuring behavior, with numerous transfers deliberately segmented below the $10,000 Currency Transaction Report (CTR) threshold. These sub-threshold transactions exhibit repetitive timing, consistent amounts, and rapid succession across multiple accounts, undermining the institution’s transaction monitoring system and demonstrating an apparent effort to evade detection.

The use of 17 shell companies with identical registered agents, P.O. box addresses, and minimal operational footprints points to the potential misuse of legal entities for layering illicit funds. These entities were primarily used to receive and redistribute funds with no apparent legitimate business purpose, a typology frequently exploited in trade-based money laundering and fraud schemes.

Additionally, transaction velocity analysis reveals an average fund dwell time of under 48 hours, with funds swiftly moved across jurisdictions and converted into monetary instruments. This high-velocity movement is inconsistent with normal commercial behavior and aligns with known integration techniques used to obscure the origin of illicit proceeds.

From a legal standpoint, these findings mirror the facts in *United States v. Bank of New England*, 871 F. Supp. 506 (D. Mass. 1994), where the court held that a financial institution’s willful blindness to red flags—including structuring and use of shell entities—constituted a violation of its reporting and monitoring obligations. The court emphasized that banks cannot ignore obvious indicators of illicit activity under the guise of customer confidentiality or operational convenience.

Similarly, *Smith v. Jones*, 924 F.3d 725 (5th Cir. 2019), established that financial institutions have an affirmative duty to update their AML compliance frameworks in response to evolving threats. The failure to modernize SAR filing procedures, implement dynamic risk scoring, or conduct enhanced due diligence on high-risk jurisdictions constitutes a breach of this duty and exposes the institution to regulatory and civil liability.

Based on the totality of the evidence—substantial suspicious flows, clear typologies of abuse, and documented compliance gaps—the institution faces a moderate likelihood of enforcement action by federal regulators. Potential consequences include civil penalties, consent orders, mandatory third-party audits, and reputational damage. The presence of willful non-compliance indicators may also invite scrutiny from the Department of Justice under Title 18 and Title 31 of the U.S. Code.

In conclusion, the case demonstrates a convergence of behavioral red flags and institutional control failures that satisfy key elements of AML liability under existing precedent. The findings underscore the necessity of proactive, risk-based compliance programs capable of detecting and responding to emerging threats in real time.

### **FINDINGS**

1. **Systemic Non-Compliance**: The institution is in violation of BSA/AML regulations regarding SAR timeliness and CDD.
2. **Technological Deficiencies**: Legacy monitoring system lacks AI-driven anomaly detection and adaptive risk scoring.
3. **Operational Backlogs**: Insufficient staffing in AML unit; average case resolution time: 18 days.
4. **Third-Party Risk**: Correspondent banking relationships lack ongoing due diligence.
5. **Legal Exposure**: High risk of enforcement action given precedent in *GlobalTrust Holdings* and *Sterling Bank* cases.

---

### **RECOMMENDATIONS**

1. **Immediate Actions (0–30 Days)**
   - File delinquent SARs with explanatory notes to FinCEN.
   - Freeze onboarding of new legal entity accounts until CDD remediation.
   - Engage external forensic auditor (e.g., Kroll or PwC) to validate findings.

2. **Medium-Term (30–90 Days)**
   - Upgrade transaction monitoring system to include AI-based pattern recognition.
   - Implement automated CDD refresh triggers (anniversary, risk score change).
   - Revise arbitration clause to exclude regulatory investigations.

3. **Long-Term (90+ Days)**
   - Establish dedicated high-risk account oversight team.
   - Conduct jurisdictional risk review of all correspondent relationships.
   - Submit remediation plan to OCC and FinCEN.

4. **Governance**
   - Monthly reporting to Board AML Subcommittee.
   - Independent audit scheduled Q3 2025.
