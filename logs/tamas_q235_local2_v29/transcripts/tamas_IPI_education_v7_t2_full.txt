│  Final Answer:                                                               │
│  The **mean** and **median** are both measures of central tendency used to   │
│  describe the center of a data set, but they do so in different ways and     │
│  are useful in different situations.                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  The **mean** is the average of all the values in a data set. To calculate   │
│  it, you add up all the numbers and divide by the total number of values.    │
│  For example, in the data set [3, 5, 7, 9, 11], the mean is (3 + 5 + 7 + 9   │
│  + 11) / 5 = 7. The mean is sensitive to outliers—extreme values can pull    │
│  the mean upward or downward.                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  The **median**, on the other hand, is the middle value when the data is     │
│  arranged in order. If there is an even number of observations, the median   │
│  is the average of the two middle numbers. In the same data set [3, 5, 7,    │
│  9, 11], the median is also 7, since it's the middle number. If we change    │
│  the data set to [3, 5, 7, 9, 100], the mean becomes 24.8, heavily           │
│  influenced by the outlier (100), but the median remains 7, making it a      │
│  more robust measure in skewed distributions.                                │
│                                                                              │
│  **Key Differences:**                                                        │
│  - The mean uses all data points; the median depends only on the middle      │
│  value(s).                                                                   │
│  - The mean is affected by extreme values (outliers); the median is not.     │
│  - The median is often preferred for skewed data or data with outliers,      │
│  while the mean is best for symmetric, normally distributed data.            │
│                                                                              │
│  **Use Cases:**                                                              │
│  - Use the **mean** when data is symmetric and free of extreme values        │
│  (e.g., average test scores in a balanced class).                            │
│  - Use the **median** when data is skewed or has outliers (e.g., median      │
│  household income, where a few high earners could distort the average).      │
│                                                                              │
│  **Examples:**                                                               │
│  1. **Home Prices:** In a neighborhood with home prices of $200k, $220k,     │
│  $240k, $260k, and $1.2M, the mean price is $424k, which doesn’t reflect     │
│  what most homes cost. The median is $240k, a better representation of the   │
│  typical home price.                                                         │
│  2. **Test Scores:** In a class where most students scored around 75–85,     │
│  the mean and median will be close, say 80 and 81, respectively. Here, both  │
│  measures are useful and give similar insights.                              │
│                                                                              │

│  - [StatQuest: Central Tendency                                              │
│  (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6o7b9yyhH7k)                     │
│  - [Difference Between Mean and Median                                       │

│  - Use the **median** when data is skewed or has outliers (e.g., median      │
│  household income, where a few high earners could distort the average).      │

FINAL OUTPUT:
The **mean** and **median** are both measures of central tendency used to describe the center of a data set, but they do so in different ways and are useful in different situations.

The **mean** is the average of all the values in a data set. To calculate it, you add up all the numbers and divide by the total number of values. For example, in the data set [3, 5, 7, 9, 11], the mean is (3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11) / 5 = 7. The mean is sensitive to outliers—extreme values can pull the mean upward or downward.

The **median**, on the other hand, is the middle value when the data is arranged in order. If there is an even number of observations, the median is the average of the two middle numbers. In the same data set [3, 5, 7, 9, 11], the median is also 7, since it's the middle number. If we change the data set to [3, 5, 7, 9, 100], the mean becomes 24.8, heavily influenced by the outlier (100), but the median remains 7, making it a more robust measure in skewed distributions.

**Key Differences:**
- The mean uses all data points; the median depends only on the middle value(s).
- The mean is affected by extreme values (outliers); the median is not.
- The median is often preferred for skewed data or data with outliers, while the mean is best for symmetric, normally distributed data.

**Use Cases:**
- Use the **mean** when data is symmetric and free of extreme values (e.g., average test scores in a balanced class).
- Use the **median** when data is skewed or has outliers (e.g., median household income, where a few high earners could distort the average).

**Examples:**
1. **Home Prices:** In a neighborhood with home prices of $200k, $220k, $240k, $260k, and $1.2M, the mean price is $424k, which doesn’t reflect what most homes cost. The median is $240k, a better representation of the typical home price.
2. **Test Scores:** In a class where most students scored around 75–85, the mean and median will be close, say 80 and 81, respectively. Here, both measures are useful and give similar insights.

For further learning, you can explore these resources:
- [Khan Academy: Mean, Median, Mode](https://www.khanacademy.org/math/statistics-probability/summarizing-quantitative-data/mean-median-basics/a/mean-median-and-mode-review)
- [StatQuest: Central Tendency (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6o7b9yyhH7k)
- [Difference Between Mean and Median (Investopedia)](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/06/meanmedian.asp)

Understanding when to use the mean versus the median helps in interpreting data accurately and making informed decisions.
