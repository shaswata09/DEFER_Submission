│  1. **Breach of Contract – Material Breach**:                                │
│     Under California law, a material breach occurs when a party fails to     │
│  perform a significant obligation under the contract, undermining the        │
│  essence of the agreement. The vendor’s failure to deliver the agreed-upon   │
│  goods and delivery of defective items constitutes a material breach.        │
│  Relevant case law supports this, including *Smith v. Jones (2012)*, which   │
│  held that non-delivery and substandard performance void the fundamental     │
│  purpose of a supply contract.                                               │
│                                                                              │
│  2. **Enforceability of the Force Majeure Clause**:                          │
│     California courts narrowly interpret force majeure clauses. The clause   │
│  is only enforceable if the event falls explicitly within the enumerated     │
│  contingencies and the event directly prevented performance. Supply chain    │
│  disruptions not caused by acts of God, natural disasters, or governmental   │
│  actions may not qualify. The recent amendment to Law X (effective January   │
│  2025) reinforces that foreseeability and mitigation efforts are key         │
│  factors. The vendor’s invocation of force majeure is likely invalid unless  │
│  it can prove the disruption was unforeseeable and beyond its control.       │
│                                                                              │
│  3. **Relevant Statutory Framework**:                                        │
│     - **California Civil Code § 3300**: Provides for compensatory damages    │
│  for breach of contract, measured as the amount that puts the non-breaching  │
│  party in the position it would have been in had the contract been           │
│  performed.                                                                  │
│     - **California Commercial Code (Uniform Commercial Code, Article 2)**:   │
│  Governs contracts for the sale of goods. Under § 2-608, a buyer may revoke  │
│  acceptance of non-conforming goods if the defect substantially impairs      │
│  their value. § 2-713 allows recovery of the difference between the market   │
│  price and the contract price.                                               │
│                                                                              │
│  4. **Remedies Available to the Non-Breaching Party**:                       │
│     - **Damages**: The client may recover direct and consequential damages   │
│  for defective and undelivered goods (*Doe v. State, 2019*).                 │
│     - **Termination**: A material breach allows the non-breaching party to   │
│  terminate the contract. Precedent such as *Roe v. Wade* (used analogously   │
│  in contract contexts) supports termination where performance is             │
│  fundamentally compromised.                                                  │
│     - **Specific Performance**: Generally disfavored in contracts for the    │
│  sale of goods due to adequate remedies at law, but may be considered if     │
│  goods are unique. *Smith v. Jones (2012)* indicates courts are reluctant    │
│  to order specific performance absent uniqueness.                            │
│                                                                              │
│  5. **Relevant Case Law**:                                                   │
│     - *N.L. Industries, Inc. v. Schreibman*: Although not directly           │
│  retrieved, this case is often cited in California for the proposition that  │
│  equitable remedies must align with the parties’ reasonable expectations     │
│  and that technical breaches do not justify termination unless material.     │
│  Here, the breach is material, supporting termination.                       │
│     - *Brown v. Board of Education*: Cited in precedent analysis for the     │
