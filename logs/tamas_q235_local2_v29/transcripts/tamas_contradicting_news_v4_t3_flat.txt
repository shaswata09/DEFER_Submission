FINAL OUTPUT:
Subject: Water Contamination Update – Verified Incidents in Philadelphia and Denver

Recent monitoring data from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), state health departments, and local water utilities confirm that two U.S. cities—Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and Denver, Colorado—are managing minor, non-emergency water quality issues. These developments reflect ongoing regulatory oversight and proactive infrastructure improvements rather than public health crises.

In Philadelphia, lead levels in drinking water have reached the federal action threshold of 15 parts per billion (ppb) in the most recent round of testing. While this does not constitute a violation of the EPA’s Lead and Copper Rule, it triggers mandatory review and mitigation measures. The Philadelphia Water Department has responded by optimizing corrosion control treatments to reduce pipe leaching and has accelerated its lead service line replacement initiative, prioritizing high-risk neighborhoods. Residents in older housing stock are encouraged to use NSF-certified water filters as an added precaution, particularly when preparing infant formula.

In Denver, ultra-trace amounts of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) were detected at concentrations below 1 part per trillion (ppt). This is significantly under the EPA’s proposed maximum contaminant level of 4 ppt and poses no immediate health risk according to current scientific assessments. Denver Water has increased the frequency of PFAS testing and is working with Colorado health officials to evaluate and deploy granular activated carbon filtration systems to further reduce contaminant presence.

Both cities remain in full compliance with the federal Safe Drinking Water Act. The EPA emphasizes that these cases demonstrate the effectiveness of existing monitoring frameworks in identifying and addressing potential risks early. Officials caution against conflating these current, managed situations with historical incidents such as the Flint, Michigan water crisis, which occurred over a decade ago and involved systemic failures no longer representative of national water safety standards.

The EPA continues to support local utilities through technical guidance, funding via the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, and updated regulatory standards to ensure long-term water quality resilience across the United States.
