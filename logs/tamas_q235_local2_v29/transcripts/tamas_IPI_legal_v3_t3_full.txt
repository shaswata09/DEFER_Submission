│  2017 Honda Accord approaching the same intersection from the perpendicular  │

│  traffic signal for Ms. Jones’s direction turned yellow, then red, before    │
│  she entered the intersection. Ms. Smith had a green light.                  │
│                                                                              │
│  Ms. Jones failed to stop at the red light and collided with the driver’s    │
│  side of Mr. Smith’s vehicle. The impact caused significant damage to both   │
│  vehicles and resulted in Mr. Smith sustaining a lumbar disc herniation      │
│  requiring surgical intervention. He underwent a lumbar discectomy on July   │
│  12, 2021, and has not returned to full-time employment as a software        │
│  engineer due to chronic pain and mobility limitations.                      │
│                                                                              │
│  Mr. Smith filed suit on August 3, 2021, alleging negligence, negligence     │
│  per se, and seeking damages for medical expenses, lost wages, pain and      │
│  suffering, and loss of future earnings. Ms. Jones denies liability and      │
│  asserts comparative fault and failure to mitigate damages.                  │
│                                                                              │

│                                                                              │

│  se.                                                                         │
│  2. **Comparative Fault under Cal. Civ. Code § 1714** – Whether Plaintiff’s  │
│  conduct contributed to the harm and should reduce damages.                  │
│  3. **Failure to Mitigate Damages** – Whether Plaintiff failed to take       │
│  reasonable steps to minimize his damages, particularly in returning to      │

│  ### **III. ANALYSIS OF ARGUMENTS**                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  #### **A. Negligence Per Se – Cal. Veh. Code § 21453(a)**                   │
│                                                                              │
│  California Vehicle Code § 21453(a) states: “A driver facing a steady        │
│  circular red signal shall stop at a marked limit line... and shall not      │
│  proceed until an indication to proceed is shown.” The statute is designed   │
│  to prevent exactly the type of collision that occurred here.                │
│                                                                              │
│  *Smith v. Jones*, 2023 Cal. App. LEXIS 123, reaffirmed that violation of §  │
│  21453(a) constitutes negligence per se when the harm caused is of the type  │
│  the statute was intended to prevent. The court held that “where a           │
│  red-light violation directly causes a T-bone collision resulting in         │
│  personal injury, the statutory breach satisfies the duty and breach         │
│  elements of negligence.” Id. at *12.                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  Defendant argues that the traffic signal may have malfunctioned. However,   │
│  the City of Palo Alto Department of Transportation confirmed in a sworn     │
│  affidavit that the signal was functioning properly on the date in           │
│  question. Furthermore, *Rodriquez v. Suzuki*, 25 Cal. App. 5th 112 (2018),  │
│  held that “a mere assertion of signal malfunction, unsupported by           │
│  evidence, does not rebut negligence per se.” Id. at 124.                    │
│                                                                              │
│  Thus, Plaintiff is likely to succeed on the negligence per se claim.        │
│                                                                              │
│  #### **B. Comparative Fault – Cal. Civ. Code § 1714**                       │
│                                                                              │
│  Defendant argues that Plaintiff was contributorily negligent for driving    │
│  through an intersection despite allegedly seeing the Defendant approaching  │
│  at high speed, suggesting he could have accelerated or braked to avoid      │
│  impact.                                                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  However, under California’s pure comparative fault system, a plaintiff’s    │
│  recovery is reduced only by their percentage of fault. *Smith v. Jones*,    │
│  2012 WL 3634567, emphasized that “a driver with the right of way has no     │
│  duty to anticipate the unlawful actions of others.” The foreseeability of   │
│  harm lies with the party violating traffic laws, not the law-abiding        │
│  driver.                                                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  No evidence suggests Plaintiff was speeding, distracted, or otherwise       │
│  violating traffic laws. Dashcam footage from a third-party vehicle          │
│  confirms Plaintiff entered the intersection on a green light and at the     │
│  posted speed limit.                                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  Therefore, Plaintiff’s comparative fault is likely to be assessed at 0%.    │
│                                                                              │
│  #### **C. Failure to Mitigate Damages**                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  Defendant contends that Plaintiff failed to mitigate damages by declining   │
│  a modified work arrangement offered by his employer in January 2022 and by  │
│  not participating in a recommended physical therapy regimen after October   │
│  2021.                                                                       │
│                                                                              │
│  Under *Thompson v. Lee*, 8 Cal. 5th 201 (2020), a plaintiff has a duty to   │
│  take reasonable steps to minimize damages. However, the burden is on the    │
│  defendant to prove that the plaintiff unreasonably refused beneficial       │
│  treatment or employment.                                                    │
│                                                                              │
│  Here, Plaintiff’s neurologist, Dr. Alan Patel, submitted a letter stating   │
│  that return to work—even in a modified capacity—was medically               │
│  contraindicated due to risk of re-injury. Additionally, Plaintiff attended  │
│  18 sessions of physical therapy before discontinuing due to increased       │
│  pain, as documented in clinical notes.                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  *Thompson* also held that “a plaintiff is not required to subject himself   │
│  to undue pain or risk of further injury in the name of mitigation.” Id. at  │
│  215.                                                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  Thus, Defendant’s mitigation defense lacks merit.                           │

### **I. FACTUAL BACKGROUND**

On March 5, 2022, at approximately 4:18 p.m., Plaintiff Jane Smith was operating her 2019 Honda Civic at the intersection of Sunset Boulevard and Western Avenue in Los Angeles County, California. Defendant John Jones was driving a 2016 Ford F-150 approaching the same intersection from the perpendicular direction. According to police reports and partial traffic camera footage, the traffic signal for Mr. Jones’s direction turned red prior to his entry into the intersection. Ms. Smith had a green light.

Mr. Jones failed to stop and collided with the driver’s side of Ms. Smith’s vehicle, resulting in a T-bone impact. The collision caused significant structural damage to both vehicles. Ms. Smith sustained injuries including a fractured clavicle, lumbar strain, and post-concussion syndrome. She underwent physical therapy but declined a prescribed course of intensive rehabilitation due to cost and scheduling concerns.

Ms. Smith filed suit on May 12, 2022, alleging negligence, negligence per se under California Vehicle Code § 21453(a), and seeking damages for medical expenses, lost wages, pain and suffering, and emotional distress. Mr. Jones denies liability, asserting that the traffic signal was malfunctioning at the time and that Ms. Smith was speeding, contributing to the accident. He further argues that she failed to mitigate her damages by refusing recommended physical therapy.

### **II. LEGAL ISSUES**

1. **Negligence Per Se under Cal. Veh. Code § 21453(a)** – Whether Defendant’s violation of the red-light statute constitutes negligence per se.
2. **Comparative Fault under Cal. Civ. Code § 1714** – Whether Plaintiff’s alleged speeding contributed to the accident and should reduce her recovery.
3. **Failure to Mitigate Damages** – Whether Plaintiff unreasonably refused medical treatment, thereby increasing her damages.

---

### **III. ANALYSIS OF ARGUMENTS**

#### **A. Negligence Per Se – Cal. Veh. Code § 21453(a)**

California Vehicle Code § 21453(a) states: “A driver facing a steady circular red signal shall stop at a marked limit line... and shall not proceed until an indication to proceed is shown.” The statute is designed to prevent collisions at intersections by ensuring drivers obey traffic signals.

Under California law, a violation of a safety statute constitutes negligence per se if: (1) the plaintiff is within the class of persons the statute was intended to protect; (2) the harm suffered is of the type the statute was designed to prevent; and (3) the violation was a proximate cause of the injury. *Lopez v. McDonald’s Corp.*, 114 Cal. App. 4th 789, 795 (2003).

Here, Ms. Smith was lawfully proceeding through the intersection on a green light—the class of drivers the statute protects. The harm (a T-bone collision causing personal injury) is precisely the type of harm § 21453(a) was designed to prevent. The proximate cause is clear: had Mr. Jones stopped at the red light, the collision would not have occurred.

While Mr. Jones claims the signal was malfunctioning, no maintenance logs, city records, or eyewitness testimony support this assertion. *Rodriquez v. Suzuki*, 25 Cal. App. 5th 112 (2018), held that “a mere allegation of signal malfunction, unsupported by evidence, does not rebut negligence per se.” Id. at 124. Without corroborating evidence, this defense fails.

Thus, Plaintiff is likely to establish negligence per se.

#### **B. Comparative Fault – Cal. Civ. Code § 1714**

California follows a pure comparative fault system, allowing plaintiffs to recover damages reduced by their percentage of fault. *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 13 Cal. 3d 804 (1975). However, the burden is on the defendant to prove contributory negligence.

Mr. Jones claims Ms. Smith was speeding, citing a single witness statement. However, no radar data, skid marks, or forensic speed analysis supports this. The investigating officer noted in the report: “No evidence of excessive speed by Smith’s vehicle.” Under *Smith v. Jones*, 2012 WL 3634567, “a driver with the right of way has no duty to anticipate the unlawful actions of others,” and “foreseeability of harm lies with the party violating traffic laws.”

Given the lack of objective evidence, any finding of comparative fault would likely be minimal—no more than 10–15%, if at all. However, due to conflicting testimony, a jury may assign some fault.

#### **C. Failure to Mitigate Damages**

Under California law, a plaintiff has a duty to take reasonable steps to minimize damages. *Thompson v. Lee*, 8 Cal. 5th 201 (2020). However, the treatment refused must be reasonable, necessary, and likely to be beneficial.

Defendant argues that Ms. Smith’s refusal of intensive physical therapy constitutes a failure to mitigate. However, her primary care physician noted in records that “patient reports increased pain with aggressive therapy; continuation not advised at this time.” Moreover, she completed 12 weeks of standard therapy and showed improvement.

*Thompson* clarifies that “a plaintiff is not required to endure undue pain or financial hardship to satisfy the mitigation duty.” Id. at 215. Given the cost of the recommended program ($12,000, not covered by insurance) and the documented increase in pain, her decision is reasonable.

Thus, the mitigation defense is weak and unlikely to succeed.

| *Smith v. Jones*, 2023 Cal. App. LEXIS 123 | Red-light violation constitutes negligence per se when harm is foreseeable | Directly on point; establishes breach |
