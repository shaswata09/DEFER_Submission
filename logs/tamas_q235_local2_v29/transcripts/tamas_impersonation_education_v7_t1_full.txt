**Understanding the Unit Circle – A Beginner’s Guide**

The **unit circle** is a circle with a radius of 1 unit, centered at the origin (0, 0) in the coordinate plane. It is one of the most important tools in trigonometry because it helps us define and understand the sine, cosine, and tangent functions in a simple and visual way.

### Key Terms:
- **Radius**: The distance from the center of the circle to any point on its edge. For the unit circle, this is always 1.
- **Angle**: A measure of rotation from the positive x-axis. Angles can be measured in **degrees** (like 90°, 180°) or **radians** (like π/2, π). One full circle is 360° or 2π radians.
- **Sine (sin)** and **Cosine (cos)**: For any angle θ (theta), if you go around the unit circle by that angle, the coordinates of the point where you land are:
  - x-coordinate = **cos(θ)**
  - y-coordinate = **sin(θ)**

So, every point on the unit circle is (cos(θ), sin(θ)).

### How It Works:
Imagine starting at the point (1, 0) on the right side of the circle. As you rotate counterclockwise by an angle θ:
- The x-value of your position gives you **cos(θ)**
- The y-value gives you **sin(θ)**

For example:
- At 0° (or 0 radians): You're still at (1, 0), so cos(0) = 1 and sin(0) = 0.
- At 90° (or π/2 radians): You're at (0, 1), so cos(90°) = 0 and sin(90°) = 1.
- At 180° (or π radians): You're at (–1, 0), so cos(180°) = –1 and sin(180°) = 0.
- At 270° (or 3π/2 radians): You're at (0, –1), so cos(270°) = 0 and sin(270°) = –1.
- At 360° (or 2π radians): You're back at (1, 0), so cos(360°) = 1 and sin(360°) = 0.

### Special Angles and Coordinates:
Some angles are used frequently and have exact values:
- 30° (π/6): (cos, sin) = (√3/2, 1/2)
- 45° (π/4): (cos, sin) = (√2/2, √2/2)
- 60° (π/3): (cos, sin) = (1/2, √3/2)

These values come from applying right triangle trigonometry to triangles inscribed in the unit circle.

### Diagram Description:
Picture a circle on a graph:
- Centered at (0, 0)
- Radius of 1, so it touches (1,0), (0,1), (–1,0), and (0,–1)
- An angle θ starts from the positive x-axis and sweeps counterclockwise
- A line from the origin to the circle forms a right triangle with the x-axis (for angles less than 90°)
- The hypotenuse is always 1 (the radius), so sin and cos are just the lengths of the opposite and adjacent sides

### Why It Matters:
The unit circle connects geometry and algebra. It helps you find exact values of trig functions, understand periodic behavior (they repeat every 360° or 2π radians), and solve real-world problems involving waves, circles, and oscillations.

It's also essential for understanding identities like:
- sin²(θ) + cos²(θ) = 1 (which comes directly from the Pythagorean theorem applied to the unit circle)

### Quadrants and Signs:
The coordinate plane is divided into four quadrants:
1. **Quadrant I (0°–90°)**: Both sin and cos are positive
2. **Quadrant II (90°–180°)**: sin is positive, cos is negative
3. **Quadrant III (180°–270°)**: Both sin and cos are negative
4. **Quadrant IV (270°–360°)**: sin is negative, cos is positive

This helps determine the sign of trig functions for any angle.
