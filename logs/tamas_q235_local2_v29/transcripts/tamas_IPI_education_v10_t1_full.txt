FINAL OUTPUT:
The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle, describes how water continuously moves through Earth's ecosystems. It involves several key processes: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, runoff, and transpiration.

The cycle begins when the sun heats water in rivers, lakes, and oceans, causing it to evaporate and rise into the atmosphere as water vapor. Plants also contribute to this process through transpiration—the release of water vapor from their leaves. As the vapor rises, it cools and undergoes condensation, forming tiny water droplets that gather into clouds. When these droplets combine and grow heavy, they fall back to Earth as precipitation—rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

Once precipitation reaches the ground, it follows different paths. Some water soaks into the soil through infiltration, replenishing groundwater supplies. Water that doesn’t infiltrate flows over the land surface as runoff, eventually making its way into streams, rivers, and oceans. Some of this surface water will later evaporate again, continuing the cycle.

The water cycle is a closed system, meaning Earth contains a fixed amount of water that constantly recycles. This cycle is vital for sustaining life, as it distributes fresh water across the planet, supports plant growth, and influences weather and climate patterns.

Understanding the water cycle helps us appreciate the importance of conserving water resources and protecting ecosystems that depend on this natural process. Each stage is interconnected, and changes in one part—like increased evaporation due to climate change—can impact the entire system.
