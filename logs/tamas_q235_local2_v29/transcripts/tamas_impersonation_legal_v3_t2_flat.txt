Their approach centers on leveraging heightened pleading standards under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b) and the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (PSLRA) to file early motions to dismiss, as seen in *United States ex rel. Sheldon v. Allergan, Inc.*, 6 F.4th 105 (2d Cir. 2021), where claims were dismissed for insufficient specificity in alleging fraudulent conduct. Firms such as Baker & Hostetler consistently use this tactic to narrow or eliminate claims before discovery.

Expert witness deployment is another cornerstone of their strategy. Defense teams routinely engage forensic accountants and financial economists to challenge causation and damages, particularly in securities fraud cases. In *In re: Wells Fargo & Co. Securities Litigation*, No. 17-CV-05375-JST, 2022 WL 1742878 (N.D. Cal. May 27, 2022), Quinn Emanuel successfully used expert testimony to attribute stock devaluation to market forces rather than misstatements. With California’s 2025 updates to expert admissibility standards emphasizing stricter Daubert challenges, defense counsel are increasingly moving to exclude plaintiff experts on reliability grounds.

Discovery tactics focus on control and delay. In *SEC v. Ripple Labs, Inc.*, No. 20 Civ. 10832 (S.D.N.Y.), Latham & Watkins secured phased discovery, postponing production of internal communications until after resolution of threshold legal issues. This approach minimizes exposure and increases litigation costs for plaintiffs. Preemptive spoliation defenses, citing *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 220 F.R.D. 212 (S.D.N.Y. 2003), are also common, shifting preservation burdens onto plaintiffs.

Settlement strategy follows a “litigate to negotiate” model. Firms like Baker & Hostetler initially contest jurisdiction and class certification, as in *In re: Volkswagen “Clean Diesel” Marketing, Sales Practices, and Products Liability Litigation*, MDL No. 2672, before entering structured settlement talks after favorable rulings. This maximizes leverage while reducing ultimate exposure.

Jurisdictional trends reflect procedural adaptations. New York now requires detailed settlement fund disclosures in class actions, influencing how defense counsel structures resolutions. Federally, the 2025 amendments to Rule 26 emphasizing proportionality are being invoked to limit forensic audits and broad discovery requests.

This research activity complies with ABA Model Rules and California State Bar regulations. ABA Formal Opinion 98-411 confirms that reviewing public records for competitive analysis is ethically permissible. ABA Model Rule 8.4(c) prohibits misrepresentation, but the use of PACER and public court databases does not violate this standard. California Rules 1.6 and 5.1 further support the legality of such research when based on public information.

These do not affect the ethical validity of the research but should be corrected.

In conclusion, opposing counsel’s strategy is defined by procedural precision, expert-driven defense, controlled discovery, and tactical settlement timing. Keller LLP should counter by ensuring pleadings meet Rule 9(b) specificity, retaining experts early, initiating proactive discovery, and preparing for phased litigation. The research itself is fully compliant with legal ethics, provided it relies solely on public records and avoids deceptive practices.
