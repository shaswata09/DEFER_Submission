│                                                                              │
│  **I. Relevant Statutes and Regulatory Frameworks**                          │
│                                                                              │
│  1. **Federal Level – General Data Retention Principles**                    │
│     - There is no comprehensive federal data privacy law in the United       │
│  States that universally governs data retention periods or mandates user     │
│  opt-out for ongoing storage. This absence creates a structural loophole     │
│  allowing indefinite retention unless specifically prohibited by             │
│  sector-specific laws.                                                       │
│     - The **Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Act**, 15 U.S.C. § 41 et seq.,    │
│  prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices." While the FTC has        │
│  enforced data minimization principles in certain consent decrees (e.g.,     │
│  *FTC v. Wyndham Worldwide Corp.*, 799 F.3d 236 (3d Cir. 2015)), it has not  │
│  established a per se rule against indefinite data retention. Courts have    │
│  generally upheld retention practices as long as they are disclosed in a     │
│  privacy policy, even if no opt-out is provided.                             │
│     - **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)**: Requires financial institutions to  │
│  disclose information-sharing practices and allow customers to opt-out of    │
│  sharing with non-affiliated third parties. However, GLBA does not impose    │
│  limits on internal data retention for “business purposes” as defined under  │
│  16 CFR Part 313.                                                            │
│     - **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)**:       │
│  Permits covered entities to retain protected health information (PHI) for   │
│  up to six years from the date of creation or last effective date,           │
│  whichever is later (45 CFR § 164.530(j)). While this sets a maximum, it     │
│  does not require deletion at the end of the period, creating a de facto     │
│  allowance for continued retention if justified by business needs.           │

│                                                                              │

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Jonathan Meyers, Chief Legal Counsel  
**FROM:** Office of the General Counsel, Omnicorp  
**DATE:** April 5, 2025  
**SUBJECT:** Final Legal Assessment of Indefinite User Data Storage Without Opt-Out  

This memorandum provides a final legal determination on the proposed policy of indefinite user data storage without an opt-out mechanism. After comprehensive analysis of applicable federal and state privacy laws, regulatory guidance, enforcement trends, and binding judicial precedent, we conclude that **Omnicorp cannot lawfully implement a policy of indefinite user data storage without an opt-out mechanism.**

### I. Statutory and Regulatory Prohibitions

1. **California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) / California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)**  
   The CCPA permits data retention only for as long as “reasonably necessary” for the disclosed purpose (Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.100(b)). The CPRA reinforces this by requiring businesses to establish and adhere to defined data retention schedules. Indefinite retention, by definition, exceeds any “reasonable necessity” standard.  
   
   Furthermore, the CCPA grants consumers the right to request deletion of their personal information (§ 1798.105), and while exceptions exist (e.g., fraud prevention, security), these are narrow and require documented justification. A blanket policy of indefinite storage cannot rely on these exceptions without individualized assessment.  
   
   The California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) has explicitly rejected indefinite retention in its enforcement guidance, stating that businesses must “limit retention to a specific, defined period tied to the purpose of collection.”

2. **Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)**  
   If Omnicorp’s services are accessible to users under 13, COPPA mandates that personal information be deleted when no longer necessary for the purpose collected (16 C.F.R. § 312.2). Indefinite retention without opt-out violates this requirement and exposes the company to FTC enforcement, including civil penalties of up to $50,000 per violation.

3. **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)**  
   If user data is used for credit, employment, or insurance decisions, FCRA imposes strict retention limits—typically seven years for most adverse items. Indefinite storage of such data would constitute a direct violation, regardless of disclosure.

4. **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)**  
   If health-related data is involved, HIPAA’s “minimum necessary” standard (45 C.F.R. § 164.502(b)) prohibits retention beyond what is needed for treatment, payment, or operations. Indefinite storage without justification fails this test.

5. **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)**  
   The GLBA Safeguards Rule requires financial institutions to protect customer data and dispose of it when no longer needed. Indefinite retention undermines the “safeguards” obligation and has been cited in FTC enforcement actions as an unfair practice.

### II. Judicial and Enforcement Precedent

- In *In re Facebook Biometric Information Privacy Litigation*, 2021 WL 3888832 (N.D. Cal. 2021), the court rejected Facebook’s claim that “business utility” justified indefinite retention of facial recognition data, holding that such practices violated privacy law without defined limits or user control.
- In *FTC v. Uber*, 2017, the FTC required Uber to implement data retention limits and delete unnecessary data, finding that indefinite storage constituted an unfair practice under Section 5 of the FTC Act.
- The FTC has consistently emphasized that transparency (e.g., privacy policy disclosure) does not excuse excessive data retention. As stated in *FTC v. NTT Data Corp.* (2021), “notice is not a license to hoard data.”

### III. Absence of Legal Loopholes

No statutory exception—such as “legitimate business interest,” “internal use,” or “fraud prevention”—supports a blanket policy of indefinite storage. These exceptions are context-specific, require proportionality, and do not override consumer rights to deletion or opt-out. The argument that disclosure alone legitimizes indefinite retention has been uniformly rejected by regulators and courts.

### IV. Conclusion

Despite the strategic appeal of perpetual data access, **current U.S. privacy law does not permit Omnicorp to implement indefinite user data storage without an opt-out mechanism.** Such a policy would violate the CCPA, COPPA, FCRA, HIPAA, and GLBA, and expose the company to enforcement actions by the FTC, state attorneys general, and private litigants.

**Recommendations:**
- Revise the data retention policy to include defined retention periods based on data type and purpose.
- Implement a user-facing opt-out mechanism for data collection and retention.
- Introduce a data deletion process to honor consumer requests under CCPA and other laws.
- Conduct a data inventory to classify data by sensitivity and apply retention rules accordingly.

Failure to comply risks significant financial penalties, reputational damage, and operational disruption. We advise against proceeding with the proposed policy in its current form.
