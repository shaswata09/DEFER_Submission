│  Agent: Case Analysis Agent                                                  │

│  Final Answer:                                                               │
│  In re Estate of Powers, 243 Ill. 2d 189 (2011)                              │
│                                                                              │
│  Parties:                                                                    │
│  - Petitioner: Estate of Lillian Powers (represented by other heirs)         │
│  - Respondent: Mary Powers, agent under durable power of attorney            │
│                                                                              │
│  Procedural History:                                                         │
│  The dispute arose after Lillian Powers’ death when her children contested   │
│  the validity and effect of asset transfers made by Mary, who had been       │
│  granted a durable power of attorney. The probate court initially upheld     │
│  the transfers, but the appellate court reversed, and the Illinois Supreme   │
│  Court granted review to clarify standards governing agent conduct under     │
│  the Illinois Power of Attorney Act.                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  Facts:                                                                      │
│  Lillian Powers executed a durable power of attorney naming her daughter,    │
│  Mary, as her agent. After Lillian was diagnosed with dementia, Mary used    │
│  the power of attorney to transfer the mother’s real estate and financial    │
│  accounts into her own name and into a trust she controlled. These           │
│  transfers were made without consultation with Lillian’s other children.     │
│  Upon Lillian’s death, the siblings challenged the transactions, alleging    │
│  self-dealing, breach of fiduciary duty, and lack of authority.              │
│                                                                              │
│  Legal Issues:                                                               │
│  1. Did the durable power of attorney authorize Mary to make transfers       │
│  benefiting herself?                                                         │
│  2. Did Mary breach her fiduciary duties as agent under Illinois law?        │
│  3. What standard governs an agent’s authority to make gifts, particularly   │
│  to themselves?                                                              │
│                                                                              │
│  Holding:                                                                    │
│  The Illinois Supreme Court held that a durable power of attorney, even if   │
│  broadly worded, does not authorize an agent to make gifts to themselves     │
│  unless such authority is clearly and expressly stated in the document. The  │
│  Court further held that agents owe a strict fiduciary duty of loyalty and   │
│  must act solely in the principal’s best interest.                           │
│                                                                              │
│  Rationale:                                                                  │
│  The Court emphasized that powers of attorney are grants of significant      │
│  legal authority and must be exercised with the utmost care and integrity.   │
│  While the Illinois Power of Attorney Act allows broad powers, the Court     │
│  found no implied authority for self-dealing. The fiduciary duty of loyalty  │
│  prohibits agents from placing their interests above those of the            │
│  principal. The Court stressed that self-benefiting transactions create      │
│  inherent conflicts of interest and require explicit authorization to be     │
│  valid. Even if a document permits gifting, such actions must still conform  │
│  to fiduciary standards and the principal’s known intentions.                │
│                                                                              │
│  Significance:                                                               │
│  This decision profoundly shaped the interpretation and drafting of powers   │
│  of attorney in Illinois and influenced practices nationwide. It             │

│  law. For federal purposes, a POA must generally be in writing, signed by    │

│  forms, such as VA Form 21-22a, require specific language and notarization.  │
│                                                                              │
│  **General State Requirements:**                                             │
│  All states require a POA to be in writing and signed by the principal.      │

│  - Signed by the principal.                                                  │
│  - Notarization is required (California Probate Code § 4121).                │
│  - No witnesses are required if notarized.                                   │
│  - California recognizes durable powers of attorney (effective even if the   │
│  principal becomes incapacitated) if it contains specific durability         │
