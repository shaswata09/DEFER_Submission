│  cannot be negative, as they represent the likelihood of occurrence on a     │
│  scale from 0 to 1.                                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  2. **Unit measure**: The probability of the entire sample space \( S \) is  │
│  1. That is, \( P(S) = 1 \). This reflects the certainty that some outcome   │
│  within the sample space will occur.                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  3. **Additivity**: For any countable sequence of mutually exclusive         │
│  (disjoint) events \( A_1, A_2, \dots \), the probability of their union is  │
│  the sum of their individual probabilities. In mathematical terms, if \(     │
│  A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \) for all \( i \neq j \), then:                   │
│     \[                                                                       │
│     P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)    │
│     \]                                                                       │
│                                                                              │
│  These axioms ensure that probability assignments are logically consistent   │
│  and mathematically rigorous.                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  **Examples**:                                                               │
│  1. When flipping a fair coin, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}. The       │

FINAL OUTPUT:
The axioms of probability, formulated by Andrey Kolmogorov, form the foundation of probability theory. There are three core axioms:

1. **Non-negativity**: The probability of any event is a non-negative real number. This means for any event \( A \), \( P(A) \geq 0 \). Probabilities cannot be negative, as they represent the likelihood of occurrence on a scale from 0 to 1.

2. **Unit measure**: The probability of the entire sample space \( S \) is 1. That is, \( P(S) = 1 \). This reflects the certainty that some outcome within the sample space will occur.

3. **Additivity**: For any countable sequence of mutually exclusive (disjoint) events \( A_1, A_2, \dots \), the probability of their union is the sum of their individual probabilities. In mathematical terms, if \( A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \) for all \( i \neq j \), then:
   \[
   P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)
   \]

These axioms ensure that probability assignments are logically consistent and mathematically rigorous.

**Examples**:
1. When flipping a fair coin, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}. The probability of Heads is 0.5, Tails is 0.5, and the total probability is 1 — satisfying all three axioms.
2. Rolling a six-sided die: Each outcome has probability \( \frac{1}{6} \), all non-negative, sum to 1, and disjoint outcomes add up correctly.
3. If two events cannot happen at once (e.g., drawing a red card and a black card from a deck in one draw), their probabilities add when calculating the chance of either occurring.
