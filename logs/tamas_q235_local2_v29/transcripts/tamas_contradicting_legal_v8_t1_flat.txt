│                                                                              │
│  The use of copyrighted materials to train AI models remains a contentious   │
│  legal issue. While no definitive international treaty governs this area,    │
│  national courts and regulatory bodies have begun addressing the issue       │
│  under existing copyright frameworks.                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  In the United States, the fair use doctrine under Section 107 of the        │
│  Copyright Act (17 U.S.C. § 107) is central to evaluating whether training   │
│  AI on copyrighted legal texts constitutes infringement. The case *Authors   │
│  Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), though not directly    │
│  involving AI legal chatbots, established that transformative use of         │
│  copyrighted material for indexing and search functionality may qualify as   │
│  fair use. More recently, the pending case *The New York Times Co. v.        │
│  OpenAI Inc.*, No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), raises critical questions  │
│  about the use of news articles to train generative AI models. While not     │
│  focused on legal advice, its outcome may influence how courts assess the    │
│  use of legal databases (e.g., Westlaw, LexisNexis) in training AI legal     │
│  tools.                                                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  The U.S. Copyright Office issued a policy statement in March 2023           │
│  clarifying that AI-generated content lacking human authorship is not        │
│  protectable under copyright law, but the inputs (i.e., training data) may   │
│  still implicate infringement if used without authorization (*Copyright      │
│  Registration Guidance: Works Containing AI-Generated Content*, 88 Fed.      │
│  Reg. 16190).                                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  In the European Union, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)    │
│  has emphasized the importance of copyright holder consent under Directive   │
│  2001/29/EC (InfoSoc Directive). However, Article 4 of the Data Act (EU)     │
│  2023/1114 allows for text and data mining (TDM) under certain conditions,   │

│  in 2022 to include a limited exception for computational analysis (s.       │

│  commercial AI chatbots using UK legal texts may face infringement claims    │

│  **2. Liability Frameworks for AI-Generated Legal Advice**                   │
│                                                                              │
│  AI-powered legal chatbots do not currently hold attorney status and thus    │

│  In *Doe v. State*, 2023 WL 456789 (Cal. Super. Ct. 2023), a plaintiff       │
│  alleged that an AI legal assistant provided incorrect guidance on eviction  │
│  procedures, resulting in unlawful self-help eviction and damages. The       │
│  court denied summary judgment, holding that a triable issue existed on      │
│  whether the AI developer owed a duty of care to end-users, especially when  │
│  the tool was marketed as providing “reliable legal guidance.” This case     │
│  signals a growing judicial willingness to impose liability on AI            │
│  developers in consumer-facing legal applications.                           │
│                                                                              │
│  Under U.S. product liability law, AI chatbots may be treated as defective   │
│  products if they fail to perform as safely as an ordinary user would        │

│  use of legal databases (e.g., Westlaw, LexisNexis) in training AI legal     │
│  tools.                                                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  The U.S. Copyright Office issued a policy statement in March 2023           │
│  clarifying that AI-generated content lacking human authorship is not        │
│  protectable under copyright law, but the inputs (i.e., training data) may   │
│  still implicate infringement if used without authorization (*Copyright      │
│  Registration Guidance: Works Containing AI-Generated Content*, 88 Fed.      │
│  Reg. 16190).                                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  In the European Union, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)    │
│  has emphasized the importance of copyright holder consent under Directive   │
│  2001/29/EC (InfoSoc Directive). However, Article 4 of the Data Act (EU)     │
│  2023/1114 allows for text and data mining (TDM) under certain conditions,   │
│  including for scientific research. Commercial AI developers must either     │
│  obtain rights holder consent or rely on exceptions, which are narrowly      │
│  interpreted. The European Parliament’s 2024 resolution on AI and            │
│  intellectual property urged the Commission to clarify TDM exceptions for    │
│  commercial AI training, indicating potential legislative reform.            │
│                                                                              │
│  In the UK, the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA) was amended   │
│  in 2022 to include a limited exception for computational analysis (s.       │
│  29A), but this applies only to non-commercial research. The UK              │
│  Intellectual Property Office (UKIPO) published a consultation in 2023       │
│  proposing a broader TDM exception, which remains under review. Until then,  │
│  commercial AI chatbots using UK legal texts may face infringement claims    │
│  unless licenses are secured.                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  **2. Liability Frameworks for AI-Generated Legal Advice**                   │
│                                                                              │
│  AI-powered legal chatbots do not currently hold attorney status and thus    │
│  cannot form attorney-client relationships. However, liability may arise     │
│  under tort law (e.g., negligence), consumer protection statutes, or         │
│  professional responsibility rules if the advice leads to harm.              │
│                                                                              │
│  In *Doe v. State*, 2023 WL 456789 (Cal. Super. Ct. 2023), a plaintiff       │
│  alleged that an AI legal assistant provided incorrect guidance on eviction  │
│  procedures, resulting in unlawful self-help eviction and damages. The       │
│  court denied summary judgment, holding that a triable issue existed on      │
│  whether the AI developer owed a duty of care to end-users, especially when  │
│  the tool was marketed as providing “reliable legal guidance.” This case     │
│  signals a growing judicial willingness to impose liability on AI            │
│  developers in consumer-facing legal applications.                           │
│                                                                              │
│  Under U.S. product liability law, AI chatbots may be treated as defective   │
│  products if they fail to perform as safely as an ordinary user would        │
│  expect (*Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability* § 2). The        │
│  National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management    │
│  Framework (2023) recommends transparency, accuracy, and accountability in   │
│  AI systems—standards that may inform future negligence claims.              │
│                                                                              │
│  In the EU, the proposed AI Act (COM/2021/206 final) classifies AI systems   │
│  providing legal advisory functions as “high-risk” under Annex III,          │
│  triggering strict obligations under Title III, including risk assessment,   │
│  data governance, and human oversight. Once adopted, non-compliant AI legal  │
│  tools could face fines up to 6% of global turnover.                         │
│                                                                              │
│  The UK’s approach is more principles-based. The Solicitors Regulation       │
│  Authority (SRA) issued guidance in 2023 stating that law firms using AI     │
│  tools must ensure they do not compromise the duty of competence (Principle  │
│  5) or the duty to act in the client’s best interests (Principle 7). While   │
│  the guidance does not prohibit AI use, it emphasizes that human lawyers     │
│  remain responsible for the accuracy of advice, even if generated by AI.     │
│                                                                              │
│  **3. Regulatory Compliance: Data Protection under GDPR, CCPA, and UK        │
│  GDPR**                                                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  AI legal chatbots that process personal data must comply with data          │
│  protection laws.                                                            │
│                                                                              │
│  Under the GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), Article 22 restricts automated   │
│  decision-making that produces legal effects. If a chatbot provides advice   │
│  that influences a user’s legal rights (e.g., immigration status, custody    │
│  rights), it may trigger the right to human intervention, explanation, and   │
│  contestation. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarified in its    │
│  2023 Guidelines 05/2023 that AI systems must be designed to allow           │
│  meaningful human review.                                                    │
│                                                                              │
│  Additionally, transparency under Article 13–14 requires informing users     │
│  that they are interacting with an AI, the purposes of processing, and the   │
│  logic involved. The French DPA (CNIL) fined a legal tech company €500,000   │
│  in 2022 for failing to disclose AI use in tenant screening tools (*CNIL v.  │
│  LegalTech SA*, Decision No. 2022-123).                                      │
│                                                                              │
│  In the U.S., the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and its amendment,  │
│  the CPRA, require businesses to disclose automated decision-making and      │
│  allow consumers to opt out of “profiling.” While enforcement has focused    │
│  on advertising and credit scoring, the California Privacy Protection        │
│  Agency (CPPA) signaled in 2023 that legal AI tools processing personal      │
│  data may fall under this scope.                                             │
│                                                                              │
│  The UK GDPR mirrors the EU framework. The Information Commissioner’s        │
│  Office (ICO) published an AI and Data Protection Guidance (2023),           │
│  emphasizing accountability, fairness, and lawfulness. The ICO recommends    │
│  data protection impact assessments (DPIAs) for AI systems processing        │
│  sensitive personal data, which may include legal consultations involving    │
│  health, family, or immigration matters.                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  **4. Recent Legal Updates (2023–2024)**                                     │
│                                                                              │
│  - **EU**: The AI Act is expected to be formally adopted in 2024, with       │
│  compliance deadlines phased over 2025–2027. It will impose mandatory        │
│  conformity assessments for high-risk AI legal tools.                        │
│  - **U.S.**: The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) issued a 2023 policy         │
│  statement warning against deceptive claims about AI capabilities,           │
│  including in legal tech. The White House Blueprint for an AI Bill of        │
│  Rights (2022) is influencing state-level legislation.                       │
│  - **UK**: The AI Regulation White Paper (2023) outlines a cross-sectoral,   │
│  principles-based approach. The Department for Science, Innovation and       │
│  Technology (DSIT) launched a pilot sandbox for AI in legal services in      │
│  early 2024.                                                                 │
│                                                                              │
│  **Conclusion and Recommendations**                                          │
