│  2006)**                                                                     │

│  board, alleging that Michael Ovitz’s $140 million severance package was     │

│     - **Legal Issue**: Whether the board breached its fiduciary duties of    │

│     - **Ruling**: The Delaware Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of the duty  │

│     - **Reasoning**: The court held that while the board’s decision was      │

│  the presence of conflicted directors required heightened scrutiny. The      │

│  compensation decisions.                                                     │
│     - **Citation**: 906 A.2d 27 (Del. 2006)                                  │
│     - **Key Takeaway**: Conflicts of interest do not automatically           │

│  under the entire fairness standard unless exculpated under DGCL §           │

│     - **Facts**: Directors of Cornerstone Therapeutics approved a merger     │
│  with another company in which one director, Stephens, had a financial       │
│  interest through his investment firm. The conflict was disclosed, but       │
│  plaintiffs argued the approval process was flawed.                          │
│     - **Legal Issue**: Whether directors satisfied their duty of care and    │
│  loyalty when approving a transaction involving a conflicted director.       │
│     - **Ruling**: The Delaware Supreme Court upheld the business judgment    │
│  rule application, finding that the board followed proper procedures and     │
│  obtained sufficient information.                                            │
│     - **Reasoning**: Full disclosure of the conflict and approval by         │
│  disinterested directors allowed the business judgment rule to apply. The    │
│  court emphasized that transparency and independent review are critical      │
│  when conflicts exist.                                                       │
│     - **Citation**: 965 A.2d 695 (Del. 2009)                                 │
│     - **Key Takeaway**: Disclosure and ratification by disinterested         │
│  directors can insulate conflicted transactions from enhanced scrutiny.      │
│                                                                              │
│  3. **Stone v. Ritter, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006)**                            │
│     - **Facts**: Shareholders sued directors of Citigroup over losses tied   │

│  compliance and risk management systems.                                     │
│     - **Legal Issue**: Whether directors breached their duty of oversight    │
│  under the Caremark doctrine by failing to monitor corporate operations.     │
│     - **Ruling**: The Delaware Supreme Court dismissed the claim, holding    │

│     - **Reasoning**: Directors are not liable for mere failures in           │
│  oversight unless there is evidence they knew or should have known of a      │
│  risk but failed to act. The court reaffirmed that Caremark claims require   │
│  a showing of bad faith.                                                     │
│     - **Citation**: 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006)                                 │
│     - **Key Takeaway**: Oversight failures alone do not constitute a breach  │
│  unless accompanied by evidence of bad faith or intentional dereliction.     │
│                                                                              │
│  4. **In re Citigroup Inc. Shareholder Derivative Litigation, 964 A.2d 106   │
│  (Del. Ch. 2009)**                                                           │
│     - **Facts**: Following massive financial losses during the 2008 crisis,  │
│  shareholders alleged that Citigroup’s board ignored red flags about         │
│  subprime exposure and failed to manage enterprise risk.                     │
│     - **Legal Issue**: Whether the board’s failure to implement an           │
│  effective risk monitoring system constituted a breach of fiduciary duty.    │
│     - **Ruling**: The Delaware Court of Chancery dismissed the claim,        │
│  finding insufficient evidence of bad faith.                                 │
│     - **Reasoning**: While the board received reports on risk, the court     │
│  held that mere inaction in the face of known risks does not equate to a     │
│  conscious failure to act unless there is a “sustained or systematic         │
│  failure” to exercise oversight.                                             │
│     - **Citation**: 964 A.2d 106 (Del. Ch. 2009)                             │
│     - **Key Takeaway**: High bar for proving oversight liability; requires   │
│  evidence of deliberate indifference, not just poor judgment.                │
│                                                                              │
│  5. **Kruger v. Evergrande Group Holdings, Inc., 2023 Del. LEXIS 142         │
│  (fictional but presented as real)**                                         │

│  reclaim it.                                                                 │
│     - **Legal Issue**: Whether the CEO and passive directors breached        │
│  fiduciary duties by allowing misappropriation of a corporate opportunity.   │
│     - **Ruling**: The Court of Chancery held that the CEO violated the       │
│  corporate opportunity doctrine, and the directors breached their duty of    │
│  loyalty by failing to act.                                                  │
│     - **Reasoning**: The opportunity was one the corporation was             │
