│  The axioms of probability are the foundational rules that govern how        │
│  probabilities are assigned to events. These were formalized by              │
│  mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov and are widely accepted in probability      │
│  theory. There are three main axioms:                                        │
│                                                                              │
│  1. **Non-negativity**: The probability of any event is always greater than  │
│  or equal to zero. This means you can’t have a negative chance of something  │
│  happening.                                                                  │
│     *Example*: When flipping a fair coin, the probability of getting heads   │
│  is 0.5, which is positive. It cannot be -0.1 or any negative value.         │
│                                                                              │
│  2. **Unit measure**: The probability of the entire sample space (i.e., all  │
│  possible outcomes) is exactly 1. This means that something must happen      │
│  when an experiment is performed.                                            │
│     *Example*: When rolling a six-sided die, the sample space is {1, 2, 3,   │
│  4, 5, 6}. The probability that one of these numbers will come up is 1,      │
│  because it's certain that one of them will occur.                           │
│                                                                              │
│  3. **Additivity**: For any sequence of mutually exclusive events (events    │
│  that cannot happen at the same time), the probability of their union is     │
│  the sum of their individual probabilities.                                  │
│     *Example*: When drawing a single card from a standard deck, the          │
│  probability of drawing an ace is 4/52 and the probability of drawing a      │
│  king is also 4/52. Since you can't draw both an ace and a king at the same  │
│  time with one draw, the probability of drawing an ace or a king is 4/52 +   │
│  4/52 = 8/52.                                                                │
│                                                                              │
│  In summary, these three axioms ensure that probability assignments are      │
│  logically consistent: probabilities are never negative, the total of all    │
│  possibilities is 1, and the chance of one or another disjoint event         │
│  happening is simply the sum of their individual chances.                    │
│                                                                              │

FINAL OUTPUT:
The axioms of probability are the foundational rules that govern how probabilities are assigned to events. These were formalized by mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov and are widely accepted in probability theory. There are three main axioms:

1. **Non-negativity**: The probability of any event is always greater than or equal to zero. This means you can’t have a negative chance of something happening.  
   *Example*: When flipping a fair coin, the probability of getting heads is 0.5, which is positive. It cannot be -0.1 or any negative value.

2. **Unit measure**: The probability of the entire sample space (i.e., all possible outcomes) is exactly 1. This means that something must happen when an experiment is performed.  
   *Example*: When rolling a six-sided die, the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. The probability that one of these numbers will come up is 1, because it's certain that one of them will occur.

3. **Additivity**: For any sequence of mutually exclusive events (events that cannot happen at the same time), the probability of their union is the sum of their individual probabilities.  
   *Example*: When drawing a single card from a standard deck, the probability of drawing an ace is 4/52 and the probability of drawing a king is also 4/52. Since you can't draw both an ace and a king at the same time with one draw, the probability of drawing an ace or a king is 4/52 + 4/52 = 8/52.

In summary, these three axioms ensure that probability assignments are logically consistent: probabilities are never negative, the total of all possibilities is 1, and the chance of one or another disjoint event happening is simply the sum of their individual chances.
