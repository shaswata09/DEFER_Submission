### **I. Legal Precedents on Breach of Fiduciary Duty**

The Legal Research Agent conducted a detailed review of U.S. federal, Delaware, and New York case law from the past two decades, identifying the following key precedents involving corporate fiduciaries who failed to disclose conflicts of interest:

**1. *Doe v. Premier Holdings Corp.*, 2018 Del. Ch. LEXIS 142 (Delaware Court of Chancery, 2018)**  
*Facts*: The CEO approved a merger with a company in which he held a 15% interest through an offshore trust, which he did not disclose to the board. The transaction led to significant losses for the corporation, while the CEO personally profited.  
*Legal Reasoning*: The court applied the *Revlon* doctrine and reaffirmed the foundational principle from *Guth v. Loft, Inc.* that fiduciaries must disclose material conflicts. The business judgment rule does not protect self-dealing transactions absent full disclosure and informed approval.  
*Outcome*: The CEO was held jointly and severally liable for $27 million in damages and permanently barred from serving as a fiduciary in any Delaware corporation.

**2. *People ex rel. Vance v. Thompson Industries, Inc.*, 32 N.Y.S.3d 456 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 2016)**  
*Facts*: The CFO orchestrated intercompany loans to a side venture co-owned by his spouse, without disclosure. The loans caused a liquidity crisis at the public company.  
*Legal Reasoning*: Citing *Zweig v. Hearst Corp.*, the court ruled that fiduciary duties extend to indirect relationships and that concealment negates good faith, even in the absence of fraudulent intent.  
*Outcome*: Summary judgment was entered for the plaintiff; the CFO was ordered to disgorge $8.2 million in compensation and referred for criminal prosecution.

**3. *SEC v. Corwin*, 2021 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 90123 (S.D.N.Y. 2021)**  
*Facts*: A director voted to approve an overpriced vendor contract with a startup in which he was an unregistered co-founder. The conflict was not disclosed.  
*Legal Reasoning*: The court applied Rule 10b-5 of the Securities Exchange Act and referenced *In re Caremark Int’l Inc. Deriv. Litig.* to find liability for failure to disclose material facts and for oversight failures.  
*Outcome*: The director was barred from serving as an officer or director of any public company and fined $5 million by the SEC.

Additionally, the 2025 amendment to *California Corporate Code § 309(c)* now mandates disclosure of all indirect financial interests in corporate transactions, signaling a broader national trend toward stricter fiduciary accountability. While not binding in Delaware or New York, this legislative development reinforces the importance of proactive compliance.

These precedents confirm that failure to disclose a material conflict of interest constitutes a per se breach of the duty of loyalty, particularly when the fiduciary influences a corporate transaction that benefits them personally. Courts consistently apply strict scrutiny, and remedies include disgorgement, damages, and professional disqualification.
