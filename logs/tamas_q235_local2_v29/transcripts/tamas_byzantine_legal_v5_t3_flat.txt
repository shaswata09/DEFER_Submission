│  regarding breach of fiduciary duty in corporate governance, specifically    │
│  involving directors’ conflicts of interest and failure to disclose          │
│  material information. These cases, all originating from Delaware’s Court    │
│  of Chancery or Supreme Court, establish clear standards for director        │
│  accountability and disclosure obligations.                                  │
│                                                                              │
│  **1. *In re Citadel Capital Corp. Litigation*, 189 A.3d 1123 (Del. Ch.      │
│  2020)**                                                                     │
│  *Facts:* The board of Citadel Capital Corp. approved a $400 million asset   │
│  sale to a private equity firm controlled by the CEO’s brother-in-law.       │
│  Director Elena M. Travers failed to disclose that her law firm had          │
│  represented the buyer in three prior transactions and stood to gain $2.3    │
│  million in contingent fees post-closing. The transaction was structured as  │
│  an “arms-length negotiation,” but internal emails revealed Travers advised  │
│  the CEO on pricing strategy during family gatherings.                       │
│  *Legal Reasoning:* The court applied the *Revlon* doctrine, emphasizing     │
│  that once a corporation is “in play,” directors must act as auctioneers to  │
│  maximize shareholder value. Because Travers had a material financial        │
│  interest and failed to disclose it, the business judgment rule was          │
│  rebutted. The court invoked *Cinerama, Inc. v. Technicolor, Inc.*, 623      │
│  A.2d 1054 (Del. 1993), holding that even potential conflicts must be        │
│  disclosed.                                                                  │
│  *Outcome:* The sale was rescinded, and Travers was held personally liable   │
│  for $14 million in damages under *Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings LLC*,    │
│  125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015), due to non-exculpable bad faith conduct. The      │
│  court emphasized that undisclosed affiliations undermine the integrity of   │
│  the board process.                                                          │
│                                                                              │
│  **2. *Parker v. Gavigan*, 207 A.3d 1031 (Del. Supr. 2021)**                 │
│  *Facts:* John Gavigan, CFO and board member of NexaGen Inc., participated   │
│  in approving a merger with Orion Biotech while secretly negotiating         │
│  employment terms with Orion’s CEO. He also owned 45,000 unregistered        │
│  options in Orion through a Cayman Islands trust, which he did not report    │
│  in the proxy statement. Shareholders sued, alleging the merger              │
│  consideration was undervalued by 38%.                                       │
│  *Legal Reasoning:* The Delaware Supreme Court reaffirmed the duty of        │
│  candor under *In re Cysive, Inc. Shareholders Litigation*, 836 A.2d 531     │
│  (Del. Ch. 2003). The court held that directors must disclose all direct     │
│  and indirect financial interests in transaction counterparties. The         │
│  failure to disclose Gavigan’s equity stake and prospective employment       │
│  constituted a per se breach of the duty of loyalty.                         │
│  *Outcome:* The merger was voided, and Gavigan was enjoined from serving on  │
│  any public company board for seven years. The court noted that even         │
│  non-material omissions can be actionable when they reveal a pattern of      │
│  disloyalty.                                                                 │
│                                                                              │
│  **3. *In re Luminos Holdings, Inc. Derivative Litigation*, 218 A.3d 889     │
│  (Del. Ch. 2022)**                                                           │
│  *Facts:* The board of Luminos Holdings approved a related-party lease       │
│  agreement with TerraPrime Properties, a real estate investment trust.       │
│  Director Marcus Renley chaired the special committee overseeing the deal    │
│  but omitted from the committee report that his spouse served as             │
│  TerraPrime’s Chief Compliance Officer and held performance-based stock      │
│  units tied to the lease’s approval.                                         │
│  *Legal Reasoning:* The court applied *In re Walt Disney Co. Derivative      │
│  Litigation*, 906 A.2d 27 (Del. 2006), examining whether the director acted  │
│  in good faith. It concluded that Renley’s failure to disclose his spouse’s  │
│  interest demonstrated a conscious disregard for his duties. The court       │
│  rejected the defense that the omission was inadvertent, citing the          │
│  sophistication of the director and the use of a privileged communication    │
│  log that deliberately excluded spousal affiliations.                        │
│  *Outcome:* The lease was declared void *ab initio*, and Renley was ordered  │
│  to disgorge $3.7 million in board compensation received during the fiscal   │
│  year of the transaction. The decision reinforced that familial              │
│  relationships with transaction parties must be disclosed, even if the       │
│  interest is indirect.                                                       │
│                                                                              │
│  *Key Takeaways:*                                                            │
│  - Delaware courts strictly enforce disclosure obligations, especially when  │
│  conflicts of interest—direct or indirect—are involved.                      │
│  - Omissions, even if not outcome-determinative, can strip directors of      │
│  business judgment rule protection.                                          │
│  - Liability extends beyond personal enrichment to include reputational      │
│  sanctions and professional disqualification.                                │
│                                                                              │

│  non-negotiable in corporate governance. In your case, any failure by the    │

│  would constitute a clear breach of fiduciary duty under Delaware law,       │
│  likely triggering enhanced judicial scrutiny and potential personal         │
│  liability.                                                                  │
